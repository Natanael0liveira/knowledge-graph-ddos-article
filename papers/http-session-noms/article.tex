\title{Calibrated Scoping of Application-Layer DDoS Mitigation\\with a Session-Centric Knowledge Graph}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Natanael Oliveira, Arthur Kobielski, Marcos Luna,
Luigi Carro, J\'eferson Campos Nobre, and Eder John Scheid}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Institute of Informatics, Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS), Porto Alegre, Brazil\\
\texttt{\{natanael.oliveira, arthur.kobielski, marcos.luna, carro, jcnobre, ejscheid\}@inf.ufrgs.br}}}

\begin{abstract}
A distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaign keeps every source below per-source
thresholds, so mitigation turns on scope: which clients to block, sparing
legitimate users. Over a session-centric knowledge graph, a binomial enrichment
test blocks a TLS fingerprint only when its share of an alarm's origins is
improbable under normal traffic, at a level calibrated to a false-alarm budget. On
generated traffic the natural scope, the cluster's most common fingerprint, blocks
$0\%$ of a botnet on five or more stacks and $39\%$ of legitimate traffic. The test
blocks $90\%$ up to 25 stacks with no observed collateral, as does an unseen-fingerprint filter at $0.03\%$, and $85\%$ at $2\%$ collateral on stacks real clients share, where that filter blocks none. A learned cross-session model (AUC $0.98$) falls to $0.48$--$0.75$ on another stack count. On eight days of four CDN endpoints,
synchronized client fleets set a calibration floor: the smallest
botnet the binomial names is $16\%$ of the busiest endpoint's window and $4$--$12$ windows on the small ones. A beta-binomial modeling the fleets lowers it to
$3\%$ and $0.9$--$2.8$ windows, at the operating point of a $z$-score calibrated to
the same budget, $0.4\%$ of clean windows. Every calibrated scope stops $67$--$71\%$ of a botnet as large as the busiest endpoint's window. A fresh day analyzed as planned in advance gives the binomial 2 false alarms in 1\,152 windows. The ontology
compiles to a count query reproducing the operator's origin and /24-pair counts
on two days, and the scope exports as STIX~2.1.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Application-layer Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks continue to grow in volume and frequency. In August 2023, HTTP/2 Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487) pushed mitigation peaks to 398 million requests per second~\cite{cve202344487,google2023rapidreset}. A peak of 201 million came from about twenty thousand machines~\cite{pardue2023rapidreset}, and Cloudflare blocked more than 6\,500 hyper-volumetric attacks in Q2 2025~\cite{cloudflare2025q2ddos}. Volumetric defenses absorb attacks of this magnitude. Detection is harder at the low-rate end of the spectrum, where \textit{distributed Slow HTTP DoS} sits. Slowloris and its descendants, plus rate-based variants that still hold connections open, such as HULK and GoldenEye~\cite{cambiaso2013slowdos}, exhaust a server from several coordinated origins, each sending requests small enough that no per-source threshold is crossed. %Modern HTTP/2 attacks share that multi-origin exhaustion property, but they operate through distinct mechanisms, notably stream cancellation and header continuation abuse. Those call for sub-relations outside the scope we evaluate here, and we treat them as the immediate extension target (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) rather than as a claim of this paper.

A stealthy campaign also shapes each session to look legitimate on the per-session signals detectors rely on, such as connection duration and route entropy~\cite{kemp2023approach}. What remains discriminative lives in the structure \textit{between} sessions: the same client fingerprint recurring across many origins, and those origins converging on one endpoint. Framed this way, the problem belongs to network and service management as much as to detection, because acting on it requires a decision: \textit{which} clients to treat, on \textit{what} evidence, and with a filter narrow enough to spare the legitimate users of the attacked service.

Existing work addresses the detection side of this problem and leaves the operational side open. A meta-analysis of 75 studies finds that 47\% of application-layer DDoS detectors derive their features from HTTP sessions~\cite{odusami2020survey}, flattened into a numeric vector before the classifier, which then cannot reason about fingerprints recurring across origins or about patterns that exist only between individually normal sessions. Graph-based detectors keep such relations: KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage} classifies the nodes of knowledge graphs built from network logs and reports in natural language. Three opportunities remain open. First, the reasoning unit is the \textit{network node} (port, IP, flow), while Layer-7 coordination becomes visible only at the level of the application session. Second, coordination is captured implicitly, in learned embeddings, so no operator can inspect the relations behind the verdict. Third, the output stops at a report, whereas acting on it demands a mitigation policy whose scope follows from the graph.

This paper addresses them by treating the scope of the mitigation as a calibrated statistical test, run over a knowledge graph of HTTP sessions. Each HTTP/TLS request resolves to an \texttt{ApplicationSession} node of an OWL graph kept over a sliding window, and the sessions are linked by sub-properties weighted by the attacker's cost of breaking each signal. A SPARQL/SWRL rule raises the alarm, whose scope is the set of fingerprints a binomial enrichment test finds over-represented against the endpoint's normal traffic, at a level calibrated to a false-alarm budget. We target campaigns run from botnets of compromised devices, whose TLS stacks their operator cannot cheaply replace, and evaluate bots that present a browser's fingerprint as the boundary of the method. This paper makes four contributions. \1~We show that the natural scope, the property most of the alarm's cluster shares, blocks users and no attackers once a botnet spans a few TLS stacks. Scoping by enrichment against an attack-free profile blocks the attack, stacks that real clients share included, which a filter of unseen fingerprints misses, and it needs no labels, whereas a learned cross-session model fails on a botnet with another number of stacks. \2~On eight days of a CDN's traffic we measure what calibrated scoping can reach. Legitimate fleets set a floor on the smallest botnet the test names, a beta-binomial background that models them lowers it several times, and in the stealthy regime the scope names the botnet that the volume trigger mostly misses. We compare the test with baselines calibrated to the same budget and check its false-alarm rate on a fresh day analyzed with the configuration fixed in advance. \3~We specify the session graph as an OWL ontology with \textit{six typed sub-properties}, three of them exercised, from which the count query is compiled. On two production days it reproduces the operator's exports window for window, and the scope exports with its evidence chain as STIX~2.1. \4~We report the false-positive rate of the resulting filter as collateral damage on legitimate traffic, in clean and in attack windows.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}


Table~\ref{tab:related} compares this work with prior art on four dimensions: the reasoning unit, the representation of relations between sessions, the form of the explanation, and whether a mitigation scope and its cost follow from it.


\subsection{Knowledge Graphs for Security and Management}

The first wave of security knowledge graphs was \textit{static}: entities extracted from Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE), reports and blogs, normalized and linked into a queryable base~\cite{jia2018practical,bonagiri2024practical}. Defensive knowledge follows the same pattern in D3FEND, an OWL graph of countermeasure techniques and the artifacts they act on, mapped to ATT\&CK~\cite{kaloroumakis2021d3fend}. Graphs built from threat-intelligence text do not capture runtime traffic structure, and how to apply them in real attack and defense operations remains unclear~\cite{liu2022recent}.

More recent work populates graphs from the traffic itself, and KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage} is the closest of these to the approach proposed here. Learned models are also gaining ground in adjacent management tasks: one line identifies device dependencies through link prediction over network graphs~\cite{sadlek2024device}, another learns attack action sequences from testbed measurements with statistical models~\cite{wang2024intrusion}, and graph-based bot detection classifies the hosts of a flow graph by degree, clustering and centrality features~\cite{aboudaya2019graphbot}. All three operate on network-level entities, and none derives a mitigation scope from what it finds. Account-level detectors likewise find coordination by clustering accounts that act in lockstep~\cite{cao2014synchrotrap}.

\subsection{Layer-7 HTTP DDoS Detection}
%, over a body of surveys that consolidate the domain~
% builds baselines from aggregated flow and session statistics

The state of the art on Layer-7 HTTP DDoS detection~\cite{tripathi2021application,cambiaso2013slowdos} falls into two categories: \1~statistical profiling and \2~machine learning (ML). The first applies Principal Component Analysis (PCA) either to flow statistics~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous} or to a per-session behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca}, the latter being among the first formulations to put the session at the center, though still as a feature vector. The second and more recent category applies supervised learning over session features~\cite{kemp2023approach}, whose authors note that the method was not validated in real time and that its output is binary, with no ontological explanation. Neither category retains a relation between sessions: whatever coordination exists is dissolved into the feature vector before the classifier ever sees it. Defenses against attacks that mimic flash crowds admit clients that solve a puzzle or spend bandwidth, and they measure the service legitimate users keep~\cite{kandula2005botz,walfish2006speakup}. Deriving a filter from an alarm is the goal of anomaly extraction, which isolates the feature values over-represented in anomalous traffic~\cite{silveira2010urca}, and of signature generation checked against a pool of normal traffic~\cite{li2006hamsa}. Both operate on flow or payload features, without the fingerprints of an application's clients.


\subsection{Session Modeling in Anomaly Detection}

None of the 75 studies of a meta-analysis on the topic keeps the session as an object with identity, target, behavior and typed relations~\cite{odusami2020survey}.

Industrial bot-management platforms do exploit cross-session structure: signals aggregated per JA4 over recent traffic feed bot scoring and DDoS protection~\cite{martinetti2024ja4signals}. They are proprietary, with no public ontology, no reproducible academic benchmark and no formalized collateral-damage metric. The channel a scope would travel on is standardized: the IETF DOTS signal channel carries a mitigation request from the attacked domain to a mitigator, scoped by the resources under attack. Its data channel installs filters on network and transport header fields, and its telemetry reports top talkers by source prefix~\cite{dots2020architecture,dots2021signal,dots2020data,dots2022telemetry}. No DOTS attribute can express an application-layer client fingerprint such as JA4.

Table~\ref{tab:related} makes the boundary explicit. Statistical and machine-learning detectors reduce the session to a vector, so no relation between sessions survives and no mitigation scope or cost can follow from the decision. Graph-based detectors keep relations, on network-level entities and in learned form, and industrial platforms act on per-fingerprint aggregates without disclosing the representation or the cost of acting on it. No published approach, therefore, holds three properties together: the application session as the reasoning unit, explicit and typed relations between sessions, and a mitigation scope derived from the representation that produced the verdict, with its collateral damage reported. The rest of this paper supplies that combination and measures what a wrong scope costs.

\section{Session-Centric Knowledge Graph}
\label{sec:approach}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/fig1_ontology.png}
\caption{Session-centric ontology and detection rule. Endpoint convergence links every pair of sessions on the endpoint. A few are drawn.}
\label{fig:ontology}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology} summarizes the framework. Four stages run over live
traffic: HTTP events are ingested, lifted into ontology instances with sessions
as persistent graph nodes, evaluated by rules, and turned into a verdict with its evidence chain and scope.

\subsection{Employing Knowledge Graphs}

A knowledge graph states facts as RDF triples $\langle$subject, predicate,
object$\rangle$, and an OWL~2 ontology declares the classes and properties they
may use. We work in the OWL~2~RL profile~\cite{w3c2012owl2profiles}, whose
polynomial forward-chaining entailment makes bounded materialization tractable
for runtime reasoning where OWL~2~DL is not, and materialization runs once per
window, off the per-request path. SWRL adds Horn rules, which assert a triple when
a conjunction of premises holds, and SPARQL queries and aggregates the result. Client identity comes from
JA4~\cite{althouse2023ja4}, a hash of the TLS \textit{ClientHello} that identifies
a client's TLS stack. Being independent of the source address, it survives IP
rotation.

\subsection{Defining the Ontology}
\label{sec:ontology}

The central class is \texttt{ApplicationSession}, related by
\texttt{hasIdentity} to a composite \texttt{Identity} (cookie, JWT token,
username, TLS fingerprint), by \texttt{originatesFrom} to its source
\texttt{IPAddress}, by \texttt{targets} to an \texttt{Endpoint}
(\texttt{LoginEndpoint}, \texttt{APIEndpoint}, \texttt{StaticEndpoint}), by
\texttt{exhibitsBehavior} to a behavioral profile, and by \texttt{mitigatedBy}
to a \texttt{Mitigation} (\texttt{RateLimitPolicy}, \texttt{ChallengeResponse}, \texttt{WAFRule}). The evidence chain carries its scope (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}). The abstract class \texttt{ApplicationLayerAttack}
specializes into \texttt{SlowHTTPDoSFamily}, itself split into
\texttt{ConnectionExhaustionAttack} (Slowloris, slow body, slow read) and
\texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood} (HULK, GoldenEye), the class our rule derives. The ontology is aligned with
STIX~2.1~\cite{oasis2021stix} and MITRE ATT\&CK.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

The defender operates the attacked application and observes, per request, the TLS
\textit{ClientHello} (hence a JA4 fingerprint), source address and prefix/ASN,
target endpoint, arrival time, and whatever identity material the application
exposes. It sees neither the botnet's control channel nor which sources are
coordinated, but can profile the service's own traffic outside attack episodes.
The attacker controls $K$ compromised devices and may shape each session to be
statistically indistinguishable from a legitimate one, the stealth assumption of
our evaluation. The damage is exhausted capacity: each session holds a connection
and a worker as a legitimate one does, so no per-session rate rises, and the
aggregate grows only by the campaign's share of the endpoint's traffic. Timing, identities and payloads can be jittered, rotated or
randomized, each at an operational cost that the weighting of
Section~\ref{sec:family} encodes. The TLS stack is harder to change, because it
is fixed by the library each device ships, as in the router, camera and DVR
botnets of the Mirai lineage~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}. Presenting a browser's
\textit{ClientHello} would require shipping an impersonation library to every
device and keeping it in step with browser releases. Bots that do so, such as headless-browser farms or bots built on TLS-impersonation libraries, fall outside this model, and Sections~\ref{sec:collateral}
and~\ref{sec:discussion} evaluate them as its boundary.

\subsection{The Weighted \texttt{relatedTo} Family}
\label{sec:family}


Cross-session relatedness is a structured family of relations, declared
via \texttt{rdfs:subPropertyOf} under a symmetric \texttt{relatedTo} and
annotated with a \texttt{coordinationWeight} $w_i \in [0,1]$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology}). Each is instantiated independently from its own observed evidence, so two sessions may share one, several or all six. Three of the six are exercised here (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

The weights follow the attacker's cost of breaking each signal. A TLS stack is fixed by each device's library, and JA4 sorts extensions, so it survives the extension randomization that breaks JA3~\cite{althouse2023ja4}. Botnets spread across prefixes, and carrier-grade NAT makes a shared /24 a poor discriminator, which leaves network proximity the weakest link.
Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} corroborates the ordering, leaving the absolute values open.

\subsection{Detection Rule}
\label{sec:rule}

Let $S_W$ be the sessions active in the window $W$. For $S \subseteq
S_W$ and a candidate endpoint $e$, define the \textit{coordination
mass}

\begin{equation}
\Omega(S) \;=\; \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}} w_i \,\big|E_i(S)\big| ,
\label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
with $E_i(S)$ the set of pairs of distinct origins $\{o(s_a), o(s_b)\}$,
$s_a, s_b \in S$, whose sessions are linked by sub-relation $i$, where $o(s)$ is
the source address of $s$. Each pair counts once, so one client's many sessions
cannot pose as coordination. The rule
\texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood} holds when there exists $S$ with (i) at least
$k_{\min}$ origins; (ii) $\langle s,\texttt{targets},e\rangle$ for every $s \in S$; and
(iii) $\Omega(S) \geq \tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$. It
mitigates only when the enrichment test of Section~\ref{sec:evidence} names a fingerprint. Per window, $S$ is every session to $e$ (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

At window scale this mass is volume: under condition (ii) the endpoint term,
$0.6\binom{n}{2}$ for $n$ origins, carries $91\%$ of $\Omega$ in the campaign of
Listing~\ref{lst:chain} (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}). A distinct-origin threshold calibrated like $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ therefore gates at least as well (Appendix~\ref{app:window}), and the test supplies the discriminator.

\subsection{Runtime Graph Construction}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Sessions and relations live inside a sliding operational window ($W = 5$~min by
default) and are purged incrementally, following an RDF stream-processing
discipline, with materialization over Apache Jena Fuseki and TDB2 storage in the prototype. The evaluation computes the same rule from class counts (Appendices~\ref{app:cost} and~\ref{app:window}).
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms} gives the construction algorithm and the per-pair
procedure of each \texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation. Exact JA4, endpoint and prefix
matches are equivalence relations, so each partitions $S$ into classes and
$|E_i(S)| = \sum_k \binom{n_k}{2}$ over their sizes $n_k$ in origins. Admitting a
session then adds its origin to one class per such sub-relation, a hash-set
insertion independent of $|S_W|$, and $\Omega(S)$ needs no pair edge. Only the non-transitive relations
(near-variant JA4, temporal, payload) compare candidate pairs, which prefix
indexes and locality-sensitive hashing restrict. Section~\ref{sec:latency} measures both paths against window occupancy. SWRL
states each \texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation as a Horn rule over the predicates
the evidence extractors produce, numeric ones such as the dynamic time warping
(DTW) distance entering as a built-in comparison against a threshold. Since SWRL cannot sum, a SPARQL query aggregates the class sizes, weighted by the \texttt{coordinationWeight} annotations (Listing~\ref{lst:rules}).

\subsection{Evidence Chain and Derived Mitigation Scope}
\label{sec:evidence}

When the rule fires, the engine emits the satisfied rule, the instances
involved, the decomposition of $\Omega(S)$ per sub-relation, and the derived
mitigation scope. We export it as JSON-LD and as
STIX~2.1, an \textit{indicator} plus a \textit{course-of-action} linked by
\textit{mitigates}, for SIEM and SOAR ingestion.

How the scope is chosen decides whether the verdict is useful or harmful, and the
frequency-based choice fails. The modal fingerprint of the cluster selects whatever is
common, and on a service under attack what is common is the legitimate
population. Against a botnet spread over several TLS stacks, each attacker stack
is smaller than the head of the benign distribution, so the modal value is a
\textit{legitimate} fingerprint and the resulting filter blocks users and no
attackers (Section~\ref{sec:collateral}).

We therefore select the discriminator by \textbf{enrichment}. Let $c(f)$ be
the number of origins of the fired cluster that present fingerprint $f$,
$n = \sum_f c(f)$, and $b(f)$ the prevalence of $f$, plus $1/N$, among the $N$
origin--fingerprint pairs of a profile of normal traffic kept by the operator
outside attack episodes. The scope admits every $f$
that is enriched, $c(f)/n \geq \rho\, b(f)$, and improbable under the background,
$\Pr[X \geq c(f)] < \lambda_e/|F|$ for $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, b(f))$, where the
Bonferroni divisor $|F|$ counts the fingerprints of profile and cluster.
The result, a \textit{set} of fingerprints, covers a fragmented botnet. Fleets of
legitimate clients that switch on together break the binomial's independence on production traffic (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). The level $\lambda_e$ is therefore calibrated per endpoint $e$ as $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is: the largest value up to $0.01$ at which the scope names a filter in at most $1\%$ of attack-free windows. This gives the test a floor: among $n$ origins it names a stack only past the smallest count $c$ with $\Pr[X \geq c] < \lambda_e/|F|$, so the smallest nameable botnet grows as the level falls (Section~\ref{sec:production}). Because fleets also make counts overdispersed, we evaluate a variant whose background is a beta-binomial fitted per fingerprint on the calibration windows. We use $\rho = 3$. On generated traffic, one origin per session, $\lambda_e$ stays at $0.01$.

The background must come from outside the attack episode, because the campaign
spans the whole window and would otherwise give every candidate an enrichment of
one. No labels are involved: the profile is what a defender sees in normal
operation. An adversary hiding inside a popular benign fingerprint is therefore
not enriched, so the framework refuses the scope and reports that no
discriminator exists.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Each traffic source answers one question. A generator calibrated on benign CICIDS2017 sessions and on JA4 measured at a production CDN carries the controlled campaigns of the main evaluation. Laboratory captures test conventional attacks and the closest published system, and eight days of that CDN's traffic test the rule's false alarms and, with a botnet injected, its detection. We then present the baselines, the ablation configurations and the metrics.

\subsection{Scenarios and Datasets}

The evaluation is parameterized by the \textit{degree of distribution} $K$ of the campaign, the number of devices the attacker controls. We report $K = 50$, a small botnet whose origins each stay inside their own rate limit, and $K = 1000$, spread over hundreds of ASNs and prefixes, with negligible per-session signal and a nearly empty \texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity}. A single source is left to volumetric detectors.

\textbf{Primary evaluation: calibrated synthetic traffic.} A generator produces
distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaigns, the family of
Section~\ref{sec:ontology}: sustained low-rate sessions against one endpoint,
with known coordination ground truth, parameterized by $K$, TLS and identity sharing, IP/ASN dispersion and the time window.

Two parameters of that generator decide whether the scenario resembles
production: benign fingerprint popularity, which we measure, and botnet
composition, which no published measurement fixes and we therefore sweep. The
first is shaped as a Zipf curve of exponent $\alpha$: real
populations are concentrated, since an up-to-date browser on a given platform
emits essentially one JA4~\cite{althouse2023ja4}. We
calibrated that curve against $6.33$M TLS requests logged at a production edge
point of presence of Azion Technologies\footnote{Measured 22 August 2026 from an
access log the platform already collects. The extract holds only client-software
fingerprints and their frequencies, with no address, host, URI, header or
locale.}: $495$ distinct fingerprints, the most common accounting for $38.4\%$ of
requests and the top ten for $93.8\%$. The measured curve is bracketed by
$\alpha = 1.5$ and $\alpha = 2.0$. We take $\alpha = 1.5$ as canonical and sweep
the range. As for botnet composition, real botnets span device classes
such as routers, IP cameras and DVRs~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}, and therefore TLS stacks. We distribute $90\%$ of the campaign uniformly over $M$ stacks, the
conservative choice for a given $M$, and the rest over one-off fingerprints, and
take $M = 25$ as canonical. An \textit{adversarial} mode lets the botnet adopt
the most common benign fingerprints, as an impersonating bot would, and marks the
boundary of the threat model.
Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} reports the measurement and its caveats.

Legitimate distributions are calibrated against the $\sim$322k benign sessions of
CICIDS2017 and verified by Kolmogorov--Smirnov
tests: $D = 0.003$ for duration and $D = 0.002$ for request count, both with
$p > 0.8$, so their marginals match CICIDS2017's. A \textit{stealth}
mode draws attacker sessions from the same distributions as benign traffic, so
the campaign reveals itself only through cross-session structure.

\textbf{Laboratory captures.} CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} (Wednesday capture:
Slowloris, Slowhttptest, HULK, GoldenEye) and
CIC-IoT2023~\cite{neto2023ciciot} (DDoS Slowloris and HTTP Flood), the latter being the dataset on which KLAGE
reports $F_1 = 84.1\%$ for \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, run through the same
extraction pipeline (PCAP $\rightarrow$ JA4 + flows $\rightarrow$ sessions
$\rightarrow$ graph).
\textbf{Production traffic.} Eight days of Azion's CDN traffic, as counts of distinct origins, connections and same-/24 pairs per fingerprint and five-minute window, and a ninth analyzed with every choice fixed in advance (Section~\ref{sec:production}).

\subsection{Baselines, Configurations and Metrics}

Each ablation configuration is run with $n = 30$ distinct seeds, each campaign split 70/30 into training and test sessions, giving bootstrap intervals and paired tests. All configurations consume the same traffic attributes, so the ablation isolates \textit{representation}. $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is fixed per scenario at the
99th percentile of $\Omega$ over legitimate clusters, and $\lambda_e$ so that the
scope names a filter in at most $1\%$ of them.

The per-session baseline is deliberately \textbf{strong}: a Random Forest (RF) over all eight to nine available flow attributes (request count and duration, byte and packet sums, inter-request time). To keep the negative result from depending on one
hypothesis class, we repeat it with histogram gradient boosting, a multilayer
perceptron and logistic regression over the same features. We also reimplement
three academic baselines: PCA profiling~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous}, PCA followed by $k$-means clustering over a behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca}, and supervised RF/SVM over session
features~\cite{kemp2023approach}. The four ablation configurations are:
\textbf{(a)} ML baseline without ontology; \textbf{(b)} ontology with \textit{no}
\texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation, sessions as isolated nodes; \textbf{(c)}
ontology with \textit{only} \texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity}, emulating a naive
ASN/prefix detector; and \textbf{(d)} the full cross-session representation over the three
sub-relations observable in the evaluated traffic. 

The scope is compared with simpler ones on the same alarm: the modal fingerprint
of the cluster; a filter of fingerprints absent from the profile and seen in at
least $k_{\min}$ origins (\textit{unseen}); and a per-fingerprint $z$-score against the profile ($z > 3$). On production traffic, that $z$-score and one of each fingerprint against its own calibration history also run calibrated, their thresholds raised per endpoint to the enrichment test's budget, a filter in at most $1\%$ of attack-free windows. Generated botnet stacks never occur in the benign vocabulary. A \textit{shared} mode draws them from the profile past its ten most common
fingerprints, as the production injection does.

Configuration (d) consumes the cross-session evidence as \textit{features}: the
share of a cluster sharing one JA4 or /24, plus its size. This
feature-level proxy for the \texttt{relatedBy} family keeps every configuration
equal in data and classifier.

Beyond standard classification metrics we report \textbf{collateral damage}: the
fraction of legitimate traffic falling inside the scope derived from the verdict.
It is the false-positive rate of the filter an operator would deploy, which can
differ sharply from that of the detector (Section~\ref{sec:collateral}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The results follow the claims they support: the ablation under stealthy distributed campaigns, the rule and its scope on generated traffic, what the scope costs in collateral, the scope on production traffic, and the runtime cost.

\subsection{Ablation Under Stealthy Distributed Campaigns}
\label{sec:ablation}

Per-session detection sits at chance over stealthy distributed campaigns whose
legitimate users access the \textit{same} endpoint and port as the attack, so that
neither flow volume nor destination port separates the classes. At $K = 50$ and
$K = 1000$, configuration (a) with the full flow-feature set reaches a per-session
ROC AUC of $0.498$ and $0.503$, (b) $0.500$ and $0.502$, and the three academic
baselines $0.495$--$0.518$, and (a) stays at $0.488$--$0.503$ under every classifier
family ($\alpha = 1.5$, $M = 25$, $n = 30$ seeds). Each attacker session is drawn
from the benign distributions by construction, so the collapse lies in the
representation. The cross-session representation (d) reaches $0.927$ and $0.982$,
and (c), restricted to network proximity, only $0.499$ and $0.659$, because the
botnet is dispersed. Paired Wilcoxon tests, Bonferroni-corrected over four
comparisons, make \textbf{(d)$-$(c)} and \textbf{(d)$-$(a)} significant at $K = 1000$
($p_{\mathrm{Bonf}} = 7.5\times 10^{-9}$, Cohen's $d = 13.5$ and $22.3$), a
difference stable across draws of one generator.

That separability does not transfer. Fragmenting the botnet over $M$ stacks makes
the cross-session evidence \textit{non-monotonic} in the label: at $K = 1000$ an
attacker shares a fingerprint with a few dozen peers, a legitimate client in the
benign head with hundreds. Tree ensembles carve out that band ($0.92$--$0.99$), the
perceptron reaches $0.86$ and $0.95$, and logistic regression, whose response to each
attribute is monotone, $0.87$ and $0.80$. Trained on campaigns of five stacks and
tested on 25 or 100, (d) falls to $0.61$ and $0.63$, and trained on 25 and tested on
five, to $0.48$ (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}): the forest learns the band one $M$
produces. The rule needs no labels: it reads a profile of normal traffic and keeps
about $90\%$ at every $M$ up to 25 (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}).

That (b) sits at chance too shows per-session attributes are not enough:
cross-session evidence carries detection. The seven attack clusters of the two
laboratory captures, six attacks and the DoS-Other class of CICIDS2017, come from
one to seven origins in one /24, floods within reach of a per-prefix limit. On those
captures a strong per-session classifier suffices ($F_1 = 0.900$ on CIC-IoT2023
\texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). The evidence chain of
Listing~\ref{lst:chain} is therefore that of a canonical generated campaign.

\subsection{Collateral Damage on Legitimate Traffic}
\label{sec:collateral}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig3_collateral.png}
\caption{Scope: frequency, unseen filter and enrichment ($\alpha = 1.5$, $n = 15$).}
\label{fig:collateral}
\end{figure}

A mitigation is meaningful only as a pair, what the scope blocks and what it costs (Fig.~\ref{fig:collateral}, $n = 15$ campaigns).

For a monolithic botnet the two agree: $89.8\%$ of attacker sessions blocked with
no collateral observed, where a global endpoint rate limit disconnects every legitimate user. Our earlier frequency rule was validated in that regime, which is not
representative.
From five stacks on, the modal fingerprint of the cluster becomes a
\textit{legitimate} one and the rule inverts: $0.0\%$ of the attack blocked and
$39.0\%$ of legitimate traffic hit, a net loss. A more concentrated benign
population ($\alpha = 2.0$) raises that collateral to $61.1\%$.

Enrichment removes the failure, blocking $90.0\%$ and $90.3\%$ of the attack at
$M = 5$ and $25$ stacks with \textbf{no collateral observed} across the $n = 15$
campaigns, and reproducing the frequency rule where it worked. The unseen filter shares this result on generated stacks and loses it on shared ones (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}). The surviving
$10\%$ is the tail of attackers with one-off fingerprints, so scoped mitigation
trades completeness for precision. At $M = 100$ coverage falls to $38.6\%$, still
with no collateral: a stack of about ten sessions in two thousand cannot be told
from the tail of a $1\,000$-session profile, and pooling 30 attack-free periods
into the profile restores $89.6\%$.

\textbf{Two conditions bound the result.} The first is the boundary of the
threat model: when the botnet adopts common benign fingerprints, little is
enriched and the rule blocks $30.4\%$ of the attack at $3.78\%$ collateral,
against $3.6\%$ and $39.0\%$ for the frequency rule. With a stricter $\rho$ the scope declines to name a fingerprint, the correct report when no
discriminator exists. The second is the background profile. Moderate drift is
tolerable ($\alpha = 2.0$ profile against an $\alpha = 1.5$ episode: $90.3\%$
coverage, no collateral), while a flat or missing profile makes every fingerprint
look rare and collateral jumps to $77.6\%$. A fresh and ample profile is thus a deployment requirement.

$\lambda_e$ on the preceding days brings the rule to $0.2\%$, and a threshold on
distinct origins, which fires on the same windows as $\Omega$ in $86$--$99.6\%$ of
cases, halves the false alarms as the gate with no loss of detection (6 against 14

\textbf{Fleets set a calibration floor.} They push $\lambda_e$ to $10^{-60}$ on the
busiest endpoint, where the binomial test names a stack only past 15 to 18 origins,
so the smallest 25-stack botnet it can name is $16\%$ of the typical window. That
floor falls to $7\%$ once the endpoint's five or six known fleets (fingerprints the
test names in at least $5\%$ of calibration windows) are exempted from the scope and
from the level's calibration, which raises $\lambda_e$ to about $10^{-21}$. The
exemption is an allow-list: a bot presenting a fleet's fingerprint is not scoped.
On the three small endpoints the floor is 4 to 12 times the whole window.

\textbf{A background that models the fleets lowers the floor.} Fleets make
fingerprint counts overdispersed. Against a beta-binomial whose intra-window
correlation is fitted per fingerprint on the calibration windows
(Appendix~\ref{app:window}), the busiest endpoint's level rises to
$7\times 10^{-5}$, where three origins name a stack, and the others' to the $0.01$
cap. The floor falls to $3\%$ of the busiest window and to $0.9$--$2.8$ windows
elsewhere, and no fingerprint qualifies as a known fleet, so no allow-list is
needed. On the console the test days' false alarms then exceed the budget ($1.3\%$), as do the calibrated $z$-score's ($1.1\%$).

\textbf{Above the floor, a calibrated $z$-score matches the test.} On the busiest
endpoint every calibrated scope stops $67$--$71\%$ of a botnet the size of its typical window, and $8$--$12\%$ of one a tenth of it, on which the trigger rarely fires. On
the small endpoints a botnet the size of the window lies below the binomial's floor,
which stops $2$--$5\%$ of it on new stacks, while the beta-binomial and a $z$-score calibrated to
the same budget stop $6$--$26\%$. Pooled, the binomial configuration fires on $0.1\%$
of clean windows (5 of 5\,643) and stops $38.4\%$ and $77.7\%$ of 100 and 1\,000
attackers on new stacks and $21.7\%$ and $63.3\%$ on shared ones, where the unseen
filter alone stops none. The calibrated $z$-score and the beta-binomial fire on
$0.4\%$ (20 and 22 windows) and stop as many or more at 100 attackers ($54.7\%$ and
$59.8\%$ on new stacks, $23.7\%$ and $40.5\%$ on shared ones), with lighter alarms
($10.8\%$ and $10.5\%$ of the window's clients against $32.9\%$). The binomial buys
its lower false-alarm rate with a higher floor. In attack windows every calibrated scope
blocks a median $0\%$ of legitimate clients.

\textbf{In the stealthy regime the trigger misses.} A botnet a tenth of the busiest
endpoint's window sits above its floor once known fleets are exempted, and the scope
names it in every window, on new and on shared stacks. The distinct-origin trigger,
however, fires in $0.7$--$42\%$ of those windows depending on the day, so $11.8\%$ of
the attackers on new stacks are stopped. A trigger on the scope alone, examined after
the fact, stops $89.6\%$ of them on new stacks and $61.1\%$ on shared ones at $1.0\%$
of the endpoint's clean windows, and $90.0\%$ and $78.3\%$ at $2.1\%$ with the
beta-binomial.

\textbf{A fresh day is consistent with the false-alarm rate.} A ninth day, exported
after every choice above and analyzed with the configuration and metrics fixed in
advance (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), gives the binomial configuration 2 false alarms
in 1\,152 clean windows, consistent with the test days ($P = 0.27$). Both fall on the
web console and block a median $41.2\%$ of their window's clients. That
configuration stops $38.9\%$ and $73.4\%$ of 100 and 1\,000 attackers on new stacks
and $21.5\%$ and $60.9\%$ on shared ones, and at a tenth of the busiest window the
trigger never fired. The calibrated $z$-score and the base rule, the protocol's
reference, fired on the same two windows, and the $z$-score stopped more at 100
attackers ($58.3\%$ and $26.7\%$). On the test days it shared 4 of the test's 5
false alarms. The beta-binomial, built after the day was read, raised none. The compiled count query returned the day's distinct origins and /24 pairs equal to the export in all 1\,152 windows.

\subsection{Cost and the Choice of $W$}
\label{sec:latency}

Kept as classes (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}), the equality-based sub-relations make admission constant at about
$0.37\,\mu$s per session and the symbolic layer linear, at $26.4$~s for
$100\,000$ sessions against $52.8$~s for pair edges at $1\,000$. The value of $\Omega(S)$ still grows with the square of the cluster, but computing it no longer does. The AUC of (d) is flat over a $30\times$ range of $W$, since the generated campaign forms one cluster at every $W$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}). The rule ran at $W = 300$~s.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

We discuss what bounds the results and what the graph adds (limitations continue
in Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

\textbf{The gain is regime-specific.} Per-IP thresholds handle single-source
floods, and a strong per-session classifier reaches $\mathrm{AUC} \geq 0.98$ on public-dataset attacks. Only distributed \textit{and} stealthy campaigns need cross-session evidence,
and only botnets above the calibration floor, which the background model sets, can be scoped by fingerprint, below which the fallback is a rate limit or a challenge. Whether a campaign at the floor exhausts the service depends on its headroom, which we did not measure.

\textbf{Each evaluation establishes one thing.} The generator shows the mechanism
under a mimicry imposed by construction, attacker sessions drawing their
attributes from real benign distributions, so the non-separability of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} is no artifact of a weakened baseline. Its stacks never occur in the benign vocabulary, which is why an unseen filter matches the scope there, and a model fitted to one of its stack counts does not carry to another. Production traffic measures false alarms on real clients
and detection only for an injected botnet, over nine days of one operator. None covers a captured stealthy campaign.

\textbf{The scope depends on an observable high-weight discriminator.} As the
fraction of origins sharing the TLS fingerprint falls, $\mathrm{AUC}$(d) decreases
monotonically from $1.00$ to $\approx 0.74$ under randomization ($n = 10$), and the scope, which rests on JA4, loses its
discriminator while the volume trigger survives. Jittered timing and rotated
identities leave it intact. A bot outside the threat model that presents a
browser's JA4 defeats it. Encrypted Client Hello~\cite{ietf2026ech} hides JA4 from on-path observers but not from a defender that terminates TLS, as assumed here.

\textbf{What the graph adds.} The AUC gain of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} comes from
representing cross-session structure, as configuration (d) does in features (a
linear model over them still attains $0.799$ against $0.495$). OWL adds nothing to
that number or to the trigger, and the scope's test is statistical. The graph is
the specification the operator deploys: the ontology marks what each equality
sub-relation equates, its weight and the counting unit of its classes. The count query compiled from it returns the exported origin and /24-pair counts in
every window of two production days, and the JA4 class sizes wherever the WAF blocked no client. A new equality signal
reaches the query as four triples, with no change to any code. The scope and its
evidence chain (Listing~\ref{lst:chain}) export as STIX~2.1 bundles that pass the
OASIS validator in strict mode and cross the reference TAXII~2.1 server unchanged,
save the extension definition it fails to serve. MISP's importer drops the
fingerprints, which STIX~2.1 carries only in an extension, and DOTS filters match only network and transport fields, so both would widen the scope to the endpoint or to address prefixes:
fingerprint-scoped mitigation needs a standard property for JA4. Whether the derivation, which makes the verdict inspectable, shortens an analyst's decision is untested.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We treated the scope of an application-layer DDoS mitigation as a calibrated test
over a session-centric knowledge graph. Against generated stealthy campaigns,
per-session detection sits at chance, and cross-session features reach ROC AUC
$0.93$--$0.98$ only within one botnet structure. Scoping by the cluster's most common
property selects a legitimate fingerprint once the botnet spans several TLS stacks. A binomial enrichment test over normal traffic blocks $90\%$ up to 25 stacks
with no observed collateral and no label, and scopes the stacks real clients share,
which a filter of unseen fingerprints cannot. On a CDN's traffic, legitimate fleets
set a floor on what the calibrated test can name: $16\%$ of the busiest endpoint's
window under the binomial and $3\%$ under a beta-binomial that models them, at the operating point of a calibrated $z$-score. A fresh day is consistent with the binomial
configuration's false-alarm rate, and in the stealthy regime the scope names the
botnet that the volume trigger mostly misses. Triggers driven by the scope, HTTP/2
attacks and a captured stealthy campaign come next.

\section{Runtime Construction and Sub-Relation Instantiation}
\label{app:algorithms}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Runtime knowledge-graph construction}
\label{alg:kg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE request stream $\mathcal{R}$, ontology $\mathcal{O}$, window $W$
\ENSURE knowledge graph $KG$ persistent over $W$
\STATE $KG \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FOR{each request $r \in \mathcal{R}$}
  \STATE $s,i,e \leftarrow \textsc{ResolveSession/Identity/Endpoint}(r)$
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{add}(s,i,e)$; \, $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{hasIdentity},i)$
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{targets},e)$
  \FOR{each active $s' \in S_W$, $s' \neq s$}
    \FOR{each sub-relation $i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}$}
      \IF{$\textsc{Holds}_i(s,s')$}
        \STATE $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{relatedBy}_i,s')$
      \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{purgeOutside}(W)$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $KG$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:kg} constructs the graph over the sliding window. It enumerates
pairs for clarity: the equality-based relations are kept as class counts
(Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}), and for the others the following procedures decide,
for each candidate pair $(s_a,s_b)$, whether the edge is instantiated.

\textbf{\texttt{TLSFingerprint}:} exact equality of the full JA4 or, failing that,
a near variant (same transport, TLS version and ALPN prefix, at most one symbol of
difference in the ordered cipher or extension blocks), annotated
\textit{ja4\_distance = 1}, which absorbs version drift within one client library
without matching distinct stacks, $O(1)$ per pair. \textbf{\texttt{ReusedIdentity}}, \textbf{\texttt{TemporalPattern}} and \textbf{\texttt{PayloadSignature}}, specified but not exercised, match a shared cookie, token or username (JWTs by their \texttt{sub} claim), inter-arrival sequences within a length-normalized DTW distance $\tau_{\mathrm{DTW}}$ (FastDTW, linear), and payload summaries above a cosine similarity $\tau_{\mathrm{payload}}$. \textbf{\texttt{EndpointConvergence}:} the same endpoint under
path-pattern normalization (\texttt{/api/users/12345} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{/api/users/\{id\}}), annotated \textit{exact = true} on an exact-path match, $O(1)$ with a prefix tree. \textbf{\texttt{NetworkProximity}:} a shared IPv4 /24
(IPv6 /48), a shared ASN from current BGP tables, or both (\textit{strong = true}), $O(1)$ with an inverted prefix index.

\begin{listing}[t]
\footnotesize
\hrule\vspace{2pt}
\begin{verbatim}
# SWRL (Horn): instantiate one sub-relation
ApplicationSession(?a) ^ ApplicationSession(?b) ^
  differentFrom(?a,?b) ^
  tlsJa4(?a,?j) ^ tlsJa4(?b,?j)
  -> relatedByTLSFingerprint(?a,?b)

# SPARQL: weighted aggregation from class sizes,
# counted in distinct origins
SELECT ?e (SUM(?w * ?n * (?n - 1) / 2) AS ?omega)
WHERE {
  { SELECT ?e ?c (COUNT(DISTINCT ?o) AS ?n)
    WHERE { ?s kg:targets ?e ; kg:inClass ?c ;
               kg:originatesFrom ?o . }
    GROUP BY ?e ?c }
  ?c kg:ofRelation ?rel .
  ?rel rdfs:subPropertyOf kg:relatedTo ;
       kg:coordinationWeight ?w .
  ?e kg:tauCluster ?tau . }
GROUP BY ?e ?tau
HAVING (SUM(?w * ?n * (?n - 1) / 2) >= ?tau)
\end{verbatim}

\section{Weight Calibration}
\label{app:calibration}

Calibration depends on the realism of the traffic: on laboratory captures, with a
few dozen distinct JA4, optimization inverts the sign of the TLS signal, because
benign sessions share fingerprints more than partially coordinated campaigns do.
In the realistic scenario, the pool of $2\,000$ benign fingerprints takes the Zipf shape of the $495$ measured at one point of presence and exceeds the $76$--$818$ each production endpoint shows. There, maximizing per-session attack-vs-benign ROC AUC of
$w_{\mathrm{tls}}\,z(\mathrm{ja4}) + w_{\mathrm{ep}}\,z(\mathrm{endpoint\ conv.})
+ w_{\mathrm{net}}\,z(\mathrm{net\ prox.})$ over the grid $\{0.3\ldots1.0\}^3$
(125 combinations, 60 scenarios, 91\,500 sessions) gives
$(w_{\mathrm{tls}}{=}1.0,\ w_{\mathrm{ep}}{=}0.3,\ w_{\mathrm{net}}{=}0.3)$. Only the TLS fingerprint discriminates on its own (isolated $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.93$, against $0.50$ for endpoint convergence and $0.58$ for network proximity), and the weights of Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology} $(1.0,\ 0.6,\ 0.3)$
attain the same optimal $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.943$ and are insensitive to $\pm20\%$
perturbation. The calibration thus corroborates the ordering, while the medium
and low weights are not separately identifiable and the per-window rule detects
as well with uniform weights (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). The popularity
measurement is weighted per request, which if anything overstates concentration. Its head matches $\alpha = 1.5$ and its top ten $\alpha = 2.0$.

\section{Additional Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\textbf{Session and identity instrumentation.} The approach assumes sessions,
identities and TLS fingerprints can be extracted from traffic events, and partial
or noisy instrumentation degrades it. Counting origins by source address
undercounts users behind a carrier-grade NAT and overcounts clients that rotate
IPv6 privacy addresses, whose natural unit is the /64.

\textbf{Graph maintenance cost.} The symbolic figures of
Appendix~\ref{app:cost} are an upper bound, and
the production run of Appendix~\ref{app:window} is offline, over aggregated
counts per endpoint, so a live CDN deployment still needs the partitioning of Appendix~\ref{app:cost}, which we did not implement.

\textbf{Coverage.} HTTP/2 attacks and other application protocols (DNS, gRPC) need
sub-relations beyond the Slow HTTP DoS family over HTTP/1.1, and $\Omega(S)$
instantiates only the three sub-relations the generator and captures expose. Identity reuse, temporal pattern and payload signature are specified
(Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}) but not exercised, so their weights are untested.

\textbf{Dataset breadth.} Laboratory validation covers one dataset and one attack against KLAGE, plus six attacks across CICIDS2017 and CIC-IoT2023. We found no independent capture of the stealthy regime: BCCC-cPacket-Cloud-DDoS-2024 names weak application-layer representation a deficiency of the datasets it surveys~\cite{shafi2024bccc}, yet its 17 DDoS scenarios are TCP-based. RT-IoT2022, CIC-DDoS2019 and a captured stealthy campaign remain future work.


\textbf{Laboratory captures and KLAGE.} On CIC-IoT2023 \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, whose attack clusters hold seven origins in one of 143 /24 prefixes, a \textit{lean} per-session baseline (three attributes) collapses to $F_1 = 0.179$ (AUC $0.551$). A \textit{strong} one (eight flow attributes) reaches $0.900$ (AUC $0.987$) and the cross-session representation $0.911$ (AUC $0.982$), because real Slowloris, unlike our stealthy campaigns, leaves a per-session flow signature. The comparison with KLAGE is favorable but
\textit{uncontrolled}: our strong baseline also beats the published $0.841$, so the
margin is not the representation's, and the released code, which starts from a graph of unpublished construction and ships no weights, admits no controlled rerun.

\textbf{Learned representation.} Configuration (d) is trained and tested on a 70/30 split of one campaign's sessions. Trained on 25 stacks and tested on 100 it reaches an AUC of $0.75$, against $0.95$--$0.995$ trained on the same $M$ on other seeds. The generator draws every benign session before anything $M$-dependent, so cross-$M$ tests use disjoint seeds: a same-seed split would score (a) at $0.88$--$0.94$ from memorized copies.

\textbf{Explanation validation.} The evidence chains of the seven laboratory
clusters pass completeness (every correlated session and activated sub-relation enumerated) and actionability (a scope coherent with the cluster discriminator) checks ($7/7$) when counted in sessions. Counted in origins, the five of CICIDS2017 hold one to three
sources, below $k_{\min}$, and the two of CIC-IoT2023 hold seven. No user study tested them.

\section{Cost Model and Window Sensitivity}
\label{app:cost}

\begin{figure}[tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig5_latency.png}
\caption{Cost of both layers, with pair edges and with class counts.}
\label{fig:latency}
\end{figure}

Admission happens per request and the $\Omega$ aggregation once per window, so we time the two layers separately, on a synthetic window in the realistic mix: 30\% of sessions in one campaign sharing a botnet JA4, benign JA4 from a pool of 2\,000, eight endpoints, dispersed /24s. Each runs with pair edges, as the rules are written, and with class counts for the equality-based sub-relations, with medians over three repeats on one core. Both give the same $\Omega(S)$ for every endpoint, to
within $7\times 10^{-12}$. The symbolic layer runs on \texttt{rdflib} in place of
Jena/TDB2, so its figures are an upper bound, and counting origins adds one
hash-set lookup per admission.

With pair enumeration, median admission latency grows from $2.3\,\mu$s at
$|S_W| = 100$ to $127\,\mu$s at $|S_W| = 10\,000$, tracking the candidate-pair
count (148 to 6\,495 edges per admission) at $15$--$21$~ns per pair. With class counters it stays between $0.33$ and $0.42\,\mu$s from $100$ to $100\,000$ sessions
(Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}, dotted lines are reference slopes). Materializing pair
edges costs $0.84$~s at $|S_W| = 100$ and $52.8$~s at $1\,000$, within
$198$--$309\,\mu$s per RDF edge, linear in edges and quadratic in sessions, whereas
class aggregation, one membership triple per session and sub-relation and the
query of Listing~\ref{lst:rules}, takes $0.33$~s at $1\,000$ sessions, $161$ times
faster, and $26.4$~s at $100\,000$. The quadratic term thus lives in the value of
$\Omega(S)$, which counts pairs, and in the pair edges of the non-transitive
sub-relations, whose candidates the indexes restrict. The evidence chain
(Listing~\ref{lst:chain}) needs only the pair counts the classes give.

On the generator, $W$ is the gap that splits an endpoint's sessions into detection clusters. Over $W = 60$--$1800$~s on the same scenarios ($K = 1000$, $n = 10$) the AUC of (d) stays at $0.976$--$0.978$ and that of (c) at $0.663$--$0.664$. The campaign's cluster holds $1\,977$--$1\,998$ sessions at every $W$, while the mean cluster grows from 132 to 867, and the discriminative feature is the \textit{fraction} of that cluster sharing one JA4. The insensitivity is thus a property of the generator.

\subsection*{Partitioning at CDN Scale}

Condition (ii), one endpoint per $S$, makes the endpoint the natural shard key: partitioning by it evaluates the rule \textit{exactly}, with no cross-shard traffic. A campaign spreading one JA4 across many endpoints then accumulates too few pairs in any shard, and no single key preserves all six sub-relations. A two-tier design, unimplemented here, would add a global sketch over high-weight identifiers, and its recall must be measured before a CDN-scale deployment.

\section{The Rule per Window}
\label{app:window}

threshold sees an attack or a label. $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is the 99th
percentile of $\Omega$ over the windows of 30 attack-free runs, which also form the profile. False alarms are counted on 30 more, detection on the canonical
campaigns ($\alpha = 1.5$, $M = 25$) over windows with at least $k_{\min} = 5$
attackers, and a flash crowd retimes 25, 50 or 100 legitimate sessions of a third
run into one window. We spread benign starts over 60~min, since the generator

\textbf{Production traffic.} The exports come from the four endpoints of the
operator's own services whose windows hold at least $k_{\min}$ origins. Clean traffic excludes clients the WAF blocked, and the exports carry no prefixes, so the
scope is judged on JA4 alone. The injected botnet puts $90\%$ of its attackers on 25 stacks, with /24s from 2\,000 prefixes disjoint from real ones. Fleets, such as a probe fleet active only in business hours, push $\lambda_e$ to
the levels of Table~\ref{tab:floor}, and the scope alone then names a filter in $1.5$--$6.3\%$ of the next day's windows. On a clean window of E2 or E3 the rule then names a fleet, blocking about half of that
window's clients. The origin gate halves these false alarms: the eight clean
windows that only $\Omega$ admits, with the scope firing, carry $33\%$ of their
$\Omega$ outside the endpoint term, against $12\%$ elsewhere, since a fleet
concentrates on one fingerprint and lifts the TLS term.

\textbf{WAF agreement.} Of the clients the scope would block on the
full traffic, the WAF also blocked $84\%$ and $96\%$ on the two endpoints where the
scope names no fleet and $1.5\%$ on the one where it does. The scope matches
$16.7\%$ of all the clients the WAF blocked.

\textbf{Other baselines.} Uncalibrated, the $z$-score fires on $2.6\%$ of clean
windows and $64.5\%$ of flash crowds, and a $z$-score against each fingerprint's own
history on half of them.

\textbf{Overdispersed background.} The beta-binomial variant gives each fingerprint
of the profile an intra-window correlation $\phi$, a moment estimate over the
calibration windows from $\mathrm{Var}[c] = n b(1-b)\bigl(1 + (n-1)\phi\bigr)$,
clipped to $[0, 0.99]$, and tests its count against a beta-binomial of the same mean.
A fingerprint absent from the profile keeps the binomial, and the level is calibrated
as before.

Of the four known-fleet shares tried on the first three test days, $5\%$ was the
only one that did not raise false alarms. On the last two it lifts enrichment on E1
at a tenth of the typical window from $0.7\%$ to $6.3\%$ of the attackers, at 7
false alarms against 8, and leaves E2 to E4 unchanged. Smaller shares block more but fire twice as often, on lighter filters (a median $11$--$12\%$ of clients
against $51\%$).  The calibrated baselines take as threshold the $99$th percentile of the
largest $z$ over the calibration windows, never below 3. A trigger on the scope
alone fires, with known fleets, on $2.2\%$ of pooled clean windows and
$0.5\%$ on the fresh day, against $3.5\%$ and $8.8\%$ without them
(Fig.~\ref{fig:operating}). An hourly profile did not help, and $\rho = 2$
or $5$ moves no pooled firing rate by over $0.3$ points and no blocked share by over $4.5$, while a collateral median shifts by up to $7.2$. Flash crowds of 1\,000 users trigger the rule in $21.8\%$ of windows, the
$z$-score in $86.8\%$.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The reference implementation is available at
\url{https://github.com/Natanael0liveira/knowledge-graph-ddos-article} under a
permissive license. \texttt{make compile-check stix-ingest} checks the compiled
count query on generated sessions and passes the exported bundles through the
OASIS STIX~2.1 validator in strict mode, TAXII~2.1 and MISP's importer. The fresh
day's protocol was written down, with the hashes of its exports, before the day
was read (\texttt{fresh\_day\_protocol.md}), and
\texttt{make production-tables} recomputes every production number. Both scope derivations
ship side by side, so the negative result of Section~\ref{sec:collateral} is
reproducible rather than asserted, and labels enter only the evaluation: profiles
and levels come from attack-free runs, and the scripts behind
Table~\ref{tab:symbolic} pick the largest-$\Omega$ cluster with no label. Seeds are
fixed and Makefiles drive every stage, so each table and data figure regenerates
from one command, except the inputs of Tables~\ref{tab:production}
and~\ref{tab:floor}, the operator's aggregates, whose anonymized summary ships with
the artifact and is checked against the tables. The OWL ontology ships in RDF/XML with
its \texttt{coordinationWeight} annotations, and we intend to submit the artifacts to the Artifact
Evaluation track.
