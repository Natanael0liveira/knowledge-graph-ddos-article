\title{Scoped Mitigation of Application-Layer DDoS\\with a Session-Centric Knowledge Graph}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Natanael Oliveira, Arthur Kobielski, Marcos Luna,
Luigi Carro, J\'eferson Campos Nobre, and Eder John Scheid}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Institute of Informatics, Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS), Porto Alegre, Brazil\\
\texttt{\{natanael.oliveira, arthur.kobielski, marcos.luna, carro, jcnobre, ejscheid\}@inf.ufrgs.br}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaign keeps every source below any per-source
threshold, so the operator faces a scoping decision: which clients to block without
blocking the service's own users. On generated traffic, the natural choice, the
cluster's most common TLS fingerprint, blocks $0\%$ of a botnet spread over several
TLS stacks and $39\%$ of legitimate traffic. We derive the scope from a
session-centric knowledge graph instead: an OWL ontology links HTTP sessions, as
first-class entities, by six sub-properties weighted by evasion cost; a SPARQL/SWRL
rule yields verdict and evidence, and a binomial enrichment test against normal
traffic names the fingerprints to block. On those campaigns it blocks $90\%$ of a
botnet of up to 25 stacks with no collateral observed and no label, matching a
learned model given the profile and the campaign's labels. On eight days of CDN
traffic it names botnet stacks real clients also present, which a filter of unseen
fingerprints cannot, and fires on $4\%$ of flash crowds against $65\%$ for a
$z$-score. Joined with that filter behind a distinct-origin trigger, both chosen on
test days, it fires on $0.1\%$ of clean windows, where it blocks a median $30\%$ of
clients, and stops $38$--$78\%$ of 100 to 1\,000 injected attackers per window
($20$--$62\%$ on shared stacks), but about $20\%$ of one as large as the endpoint's
typical window. The count query compiled from the ontology reproduces a production
day's exported origin and /24 counts; the STIX~2.1 export passes the OASIS
validator.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Application-layer Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks continue to grow in volume and frequency. In August 2023, HTTP/2 Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487) pushed mitigation peaks to 398 million requests per second~\cite{cve202344487,google2023rapidreset}, one of 201 million from about twenty thousand machines~\cite{pardue2023rapidreset}, and Cloudflare blocked more than 6500 hyper-volumetric attacks in Q2 2025~\cite{cloudflare2025q2ddos}. Volumetric defenses absorb attacks of this magnitude; detection is harder at the low-rate end of the spectrum, where \textit{distributed Slow HTTP DoS} sits: Slowloris and its descendants, plus rate-based variants that still hold connections open, such as HULK and GoldenEye~\cite{cambiaso2013slowdos}, exhaust a server from several coordinated origins, each sending requests small enough that no per-source threshold is crossed. %Modern HTTP/2 attacks share that multi-origin exhaustion property, but they operate through distinct mechanisms, notably stream cancellation and header continuation abuse. Those call for sub-relations outside the scope we evaluate here, and we treat them as the immediate extension target (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) rather than as a claim of this paper.

A stealthy campaign also shapes each session to look legitimate on the per-session signals detectors rely on, such as connection duration and route entropy~\cite{kemp2023approach}. What remains discriminative lives in the structure \textit{between} sessions: the same client fingerprint recurring across many origins, and those origins converging on one endpoint. Framed this way, the problem belongs to network and service management as much as to detection, because acting on it requires a decision: \textit{which} clients to act on, on \textit{what} evidence, and with a filter narrow enough to spare the legitimate users of the attacked service.

Existing work addresses the detection side of this problem and leaves the operational side open. A meta-analysis of 75 studies finds that 47\% of application-layer DDoS detectors derive their features from HTTP sessions~\cite{odusami2020survey}, flattened into a numeric vector before the classifier, which then cannot reason about fingerprints recurring across origins or about patterns that exist only between individually normal sessions. Graph-based detectors keep such relations: KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage} builds knowledge graphs from network logs, classifies nodes with Graph-BERT~\cite{zhang2020graphbertattentionneededlearning} and reports in natural language, reaching $F_1 = 84.1\%$ on IoT benchmarks with DDoS Slowloris. Three opportunities remain open. First, the reasoning unit is the \textit{network node} (port, IP, flow), while Layer-7 coordination becomes visible only at the level of the application session. Second, coordination is captured implicitly, in learned embeddings, so no operator can inspect the relations behind the verdict. Third, the output stops at a report, whereas acting on it demands a mitigation policy whose scope follows from the graph. These three gaps define the space this paper occupies.

This paper addresses them with a knowledge-graph solution that scopes the mitigation of application-layer DDoS. It resolves each HTTP/TLS request to an \texttt{ApplicationSession} node of an OWL graph kept over a sliding window, links the sessions by sub-properties weighted by the attacker's cost of breaking each signal, and fires a SPARQL/SWRL rule whose derivation is the verdict and whose discriminator scopes the mitigation. We target campaigns run from botnets of compromised devices, whose TLS stacks their operator cannot cheaply replace, and evaluate bots that present a browser's fingerprint as the boundary of the method. This paper makes four contributions. \1~We propose an OWL ontology in which the HTTP session is a first-class entity, related to other sessions by \textit{six typed sub-properties} weighted by evasion cost, so that network proximity, which modern botnets break trivially, enters only as auxiliary evidence. \2~We define a decision procedure in which a SPARQL/SWRL rule fires on the weighted coordination mass $\Omega(S)$ of a candidate cluster, so that the reported verdict is the derivation that satisfied the rule, and show that at window scale this mass acts as a volume trigger, which a count of distinct origins gates at least as well, with half the false alarms on production traffic. \3~We derive the mitigation scope from the same graph and export it with its evidence chain in JSON-LD and STIX~2.1. We show that the natural choice, the property most of the cluster shares, is harmful under realistic conditions, and replace it with a binomial enrichment test over a profile of normal traffic, counted in origins at a calibrated level, which on production traffic names botnet stacks shared with real clients and rarely fires on flash crowds. \4~We evaluate the approach against a strong per-session baseline and a learned model given the same profile as the campaign grows more distributed, and on eight days of production traffic, and measure the false-positive rate of the resulting mitigation filter as collateral damage on legitimate traffic.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}


Table~\ref{tab:related} compares this work with prior art on four dimensions: the reasoning unit, the representation of relations between sessions, the form of the explanation, and whether a mitigation scope and its collateral cost follow from that representation.


\subsection{Knowledge Graphs for Security and Management}

The first wave of security knowledge graphs was \textit{static}: entities extracted from Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE), reports and blogs, normalized and linked into a queryable base~\cite{jia2018practical,bonagiri2024practical}. In the words of a survey of the area~\cite{liu2022recent}, the resulting gap is that ``\textit{it is still unclear how to implement the knowledge graph to face real industrial difficulties in cyber attack and defense situations}''. Graphs built from threat intelligence text do not capture runtime traffic structure.

More recent work populates graphs from the traffic itself, and KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage} is the closest of these to the approach proposed here. Learned models are also gaining ground in adjacent management tasks: one line identifies device dependencies through link prediction over network graphs~\cite{sadlek2024device}, another learns attack action sequences from testbed measurements with statistical models~\cite{wang2024intrusion}. Both operate on network-level entities, and neither derives a mitigation scope from what it finds.

\subsection{Layer-7 HTTP DDoS Detection}
%, over a body of surveys that consolidate the domain~
% builds baselines from aggregated flow and session statistics

The state of the art on Layer-7 HTTP DDoS detection~\cite{tripathi2021application,cambiaso2013slowdos} falls into two categories: \1~statistical profiling and \2~Machine Learning (ML). The first applies Principal Component Analysis (PCA) either to flow statistics~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous} or to a per-session behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca}, the latter being among the first formulations to put the session at the center, though still as a feature vector. The second and more recent category applies supervised learning over session features~\cite{kemp2023approach}, whose authors note that the method was not validated in real time and that its output is binary, with no ontological explanation. Neither category retains a relation between sessions: whatever coordination exists is dissolved into the feature vector before the classifier ever sees it.


\subsection{Session Modeling in Anomaly Detection}

Among the 75 studies of a meta-analysis on the topic~\cite{odusami2020survey}, none keeps the session as an object with identity, target, behavior and typed relations. The reduction is uniform, and it puts cross-session reasoning beyond the detector's reach.

Industrial bot-management platforms do exploit cross-session structure: signals aggregated per JA4 over recent traffic feed bot scoring and DDoS protection~\cite{martinetti2024ja4signals}. They are proprietary, with no public ontology, no reproducible academic benchmark and no formalized collateral-damage metric.

Table~\ref{tab:related} makes the boundary explicit. Statistical and machine-learning detectors reduce the session to a vector, so no relation between sessions survives and no mitigation scope or cost can follow from the decision. Graph-based detectors keep relations, on network-level entities and in learned form, and industrial platforms act on per-fingerprint aggregates without disclosing the representation or the cost of acting on it. No published approach, therefore, holds three properties together: the application session as the reasoning unit, explicit and typed relations between sessions, and a mitigation scope derived from the representation that produced the verdict, with its collateral damage reported. The rest of this paper supplies that combination and measures what it costs when the scope is wrong.

\subsection{Employing Knowledge Graphs}

A knowledge graph states facts as RDF triples $\langle$subject, predicate,
object$\rangle$, and an OWL~2 ontology declares the classes and properties they
may use. We work in the OWL~2~RL profile~\cite{w3c2012owl2profiles}, whose
polynomial forward-chaining entailment makes bounded materialization tractable
for runtime reasoning where OWL~2~DL is not, and materialization runs once per
window, off the per-request path. SWRL adds Horn rules, which assert a triple when
a conjunction of premises holds, and SPARQL queries and aggregates the result. Client identity comes from
JA4~\cite{althouse2023ja4}, a hash of the TLS \textit{ClientHello} that identifies
a client's TLS stack. Being independent of the source address, it survives IP
rotation.

\subsection{Defining the Ontology}
\label{sec:ontology}

The central class is \texttt{ApplicationSession}, related by
\texttt{hasIdentity} to a composite \texttt{Identity} (cookie, JWT token,
username, TLS fingerprint), by \texttt{originatesFrom} to its source
\texttt{IPAddress}, by \texttt{targets} to an \texttt{Endpoint}
(\texttt{AuthEndpoint}, \texttt{APIEndpoint}, \texttt{StaticAsset}), by
\texttt{exhibitsBehavior} to a behavioral profile, and by \texttt{mitigatedBy}
to a policy (\texttt{RateLimit}, \texttt{Challenge}, \texttt{Block}) carrying a
\texttt{scope} attribute. The abstract class \texttt{ApplicationLayerAttack}
specializes into \texttt{SlowHTTPDoSFamily}, itself split into
\texttt{ConnectionExhaustionAttack} (Slowloris, slow body, slow read) and
\texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood} (HULK, GoldenEye). The ontology is aligned with
STIX~2.1~\cite{oasis2021stix} and MITRE ATT\&CK, and also declares
\texttt{CredentialStuffing} and \texttt{CoordinatedAPIAbuse}, which reuse the
same machinery but are outside the experimental scope of this paper.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

The defender operates the attacked application and observes, per request, the TLS
\textit{ClientHello} (hence a JA4 fingerprint), source address and prefix/ASN,
target endpoint, arrival time, and whatever identity material the application
exposes. It sees neither the botnet's control channel nor which sources are
coordinated, but can profile the service's own traffic outside attack episodes.
The attacker controls $K$ compromised devices and may shape each session to be
statistically indistinguishable from a legitimate one, the stealth assumption of
our evaluation. The damage is exhausted capacity: each session holds a connection
and a worker as a legitimate one does, so no per-session rate rises, and the
aggregate grows only by the campaign's share of the endpoint's traffic. Timing, identities and payloads can be jittered, rotated or
randomized, each at an operational cost that the weighting of
Section~\ref{sec:family} encodes. The TLS stack is harder to change, because it
is fixed by the library each device ships, as in the router, camera and DVR
botnets of the Mirai lineage~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}. Presenting a browser's
\textit{ClientHello} would require shipping an impersonation library to every
device and keeping it in step with browser releases. Bots that do so, such as
headless-browser farms, fall outside this model, and Sections~\ref{sec:collateral}
and~\ref{sec:discussion} evaluate them as its boundary.

\subsection{The Weighted \texttt{relatedTo} Family}
\label{sec:family}


Cross-session relatedness is a structured family of relations, declared
via \texttt{rdfs:subPropertyOf} under a symmetric \texttt{relatedTo} and
annotated with a \texttt{coordinationWeight} $w_i \in [0,1]$, depicted in
Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology}. Each is instantiated independently from its own observed
evidence, so two sessions may be linked by one, several or all six.

The ordering is an evasion-cost argument for that attacker. Changing the TLS
stack means replacing the library on every device, and JA4 sorts extensions
before hashing, so it stays stable under the extension randomization Chrome
introduced in 2023, which breaks classic JA3~\cite{althouse2023ja4}. Identity reuse is equally expensive to break, because
spreading a campaign over many credentials undermines its economics. Temporal
coherence originates in how the botnet is operated, and random jitter can
partially break it. Payload and endpoint
signals are intermediate: randomizing them is cheap, but doing so costs campaign
coherence. Network proximity is the weakest link by construction: Mirai
derivatives, cloud fleets and residential-proxy networks spread across ASNs and
prefixes, and carrier-grade NAT makes a shared /24 a poor discriminator. The calibration in
Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} corroborates the ordering, and makes no
claim about the absolute values.

\subsection{Runtime Graph Construction}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Sessions and relations live inside a sliding operational window ($W = 5$~min by
default) and are purged incrementally, following an RDF stream-processing
discipline, with materialization over Apache Jena Fuseki and TDB2 storage.
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} lays out the two layers and the rates they run at, and
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms} gives the construction algorithm and the per-pair
procedure of each \texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation. Exact JA4, endpoint and prefix
matches are equivalence relations, so each partitions $S$ into classes and
$|E_i(S)| = \sum_k \binom{n_k}{2}$ over their sizes $n_k$ in origins. Admitting a
session then adds its origin to one class per such sub-relation, a hash-set
insertion independent of $|S_W|$, and $\Omega(S)$ needs no pair edge. Only the non-transitive relations
(near-variant JA4, temporal, payload) compare candidate pairs, which prefix
indexes and locality-sensitive hashing restrict. Section~\ref{sec:latency}
measures both paths against window occupancy.

\subsection{Detection Rule}
\label{sec:rule}

Let $V_{\mathrm{sess}}$ be the sessions active in $W$. For a subset $S \subseteq
V_{\mathrm{sess}}$ and a candidate endpoint $e$, define the \textit{coordination
mass}

\begin{equation}
\Omega(S) \;=\; \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}} w_i \,\big|E_i(S)\big| ,
\label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
with $E_i(S)$ the set of pairs of distinct origins $\{o(s_a), o(s_b)\}$,
$s_a, s_b \in S$, whose sessions are linked by sub-relation $i$, where $o(s)$ is
the source address of $s$. Each pair counts once, so one client's many sessions
cannot pose as coordination. The rule
\texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood} holds when there exists $S$ with (i) at least
$k_{\min}$ origins; (ii) $\forall s \in S: (s,\texttt{targets},e) \in E$; and
(iii) $\Omega(S) \geq \tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$. It
mitigates only when the enrichment test of Section~\ref{sec:evidence} names a
fingerprint; per window, $S$ is every session to $e$ (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

At window scale most of this mass is volume: under condition (ii) the endpoint
term is $0.6\binom{n}{2}$ for $n$ origins and carries $91\%$ of $\Omega$ in the
campaign of Listing~\ref{lst:chain}, and with uniform weights the per-window rule
detects as well as with these. A threshold on the distinct origins of the window,
calibrated as $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is, gates at least as well, and one on the
aggregate rate adds nothing, since each session keeps a legitimate rate
(Appendix~\ref{app:window}). The trigger thus flags a window busier than normal
traffic, and the enrichment test of Section~\ref{sec:evidence} supplies the
discriminator. The weights order the evidence chain and keep network proximity
auxiliary: a set held together \textit{only} by it needs three times as many
linked pairs to reach a given $\Omega$ as a TLS-linked set.

\subsection{Symbolic Formulation}
\label{sec:symbolic}

Reasoning is symbolic in two stages (Listing~\ref{lst:rules}). SWRL states each
\texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation as a Horn rule over the predicates the evidence
extractors produce; numeric ones such as DTW distance and payload cosine
similarity enter as a built-in comparison against their threshold. At runtime the
equality-based rules are evaluated from class sizes, with no pair edge
materialized, and the aggregation, which SWRL cannot express since it cannot
sum, is a SPARQL query that reads the weights from the \texttt{coordinationWeight}
annotations of the ontology.


\subsection{Evidence Chain and Derived Mitigation Scope}
\label{sec:evidence}

When the rule fires, the engine emits the satisfied rule, the instances
involved, the decomposition of $\Omega(S)$ per sub-relation, and the derived
mitigation scope. We export it as JSON-LD and as
STIX~2.1, an \textit{indicator} plus a \textit{course-of-action} linked by
\textit{mitigates}, for SIEM and SOAR ingestion.

How the scope is chosen decides whether the verdict is useful or harmful, and the
frequency-based choice fails. The modal fingerprint of the cluster selects whatever is
common, and on a service under attack what is common is the legitimate
population. Against a botnet spread over several TLS stacks, each attacker stack
is smaller than the head of the benign distribution, so the modal value is a
\textit{legitimate} fingerprint and the resulting filter blocks users and no
attackers (Section~\ref{sec:collateral}).

We therefore select the discriminator by \textbf{enrichment}. Let $c(f)$ be
the number of origins of the fired cluster that present fingerprint $f$,
$n = \sum_f c(f)$, and $b(f)$ the prevalence of $f$, plus $1/N$, among the $N$
origin--fingerprint pairs of a profile of normal traffic kept by the operator
outside attack episodes. The scope admits every $f$
that is enriched, $c(f)/n \geq \rho\, b(f)$, and improbable under the background,
$\Pr[X \geq c(f)] < \lambda_e/|F|$ for $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, b(f))$, where the
Bonferroni divisor $|F|$ counts the fingerprints of profile and cluster.
The result, a \textit{set} of fingerprints, covers a fragmented botnet. Fleets of
legitimate clients that switch on together break the binomial's independence on
production traffic (Appendix~\ref{app:window}), so the level $\lambda_e$ is
calibrated per endpoint $e$ as $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is: the largest value up
to $0.01$ at which the scope names a filter in at most $1\%$ of attack-free
windows. We use $\rho = 3$; on generated traffic, where each session has its own
origin, $\lambda_e$ stays at $0.01$.

The background must come from outside the attack episode, because the campaign
spans the whole window and would otherwise give every candidate an enrichment of
one. No labels are involved: the profile is what a defender sees in normal
operation. An adversary hiding inside a popular benign fingerprint is therefore
not enriched, so the framework refuses the scope and reports that no
discriminator exists.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Each traffic source answers one question. A generator calibrated on benign CICIDS2017 sessions and on JA4 measured at a production CDN carries the controlled campaigns of the main evaluation, laboratory captures test conventional attacks and the closest published system, and eight days of that CDN's traffic test the rule's false alarms and, with a botnet injected, its detection. We then present the baselines, the ablation configurations and the metrics.

\subsection{Scenarios and Datasets}

The central hypothesis has two parts: cross-session representation gains over
per-session detection as the \textit{degree of distribution} $K$ of the campaign
grows, and the symbolic path turns that structure into an auditable,
low-collateral decision, so the evaluation is parameterized by $K$.
\textbf{Scenario~A} ($K=1$), the classic single-source flood, is handled by
volumetric detectors and degenerate for the per-session area under the ROC curve
(AUC). \textbf{Scenario~B} ($10 \leq K \leq 100$) models small
botnets whose origins each stay inside their own rate limit, so network
proximity is partial and high-weight signals must carry detection.
\textbf{Scenario~C} ($K \geq 1000$) spreads the campaign over hundreds of ASNs
and prefixes, leaving negligible per-session signal and a nearly empty
\texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity}; this is where the contribution must hold. We
report B ($K=50$) and C ($K=1000$).

\textbf{Primary evaluation: calibrated synthetic traffic.} A generator produces
distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaigns, the family of
Section~\ref{sec:ontology}: sustained low-rate sessions against one endpoint,
with known coordination ground truth, parameterized by $K$,
by the fraction of origins sharing TLS signature and identity, by IP/ASN
dispersion and by the time window.

Two parameters of that generator decide whether the scenario resembles
production: benign fingerprint popularity, which we measure, and botnet
composition, which no published measurement fixes and we therefore sweep. The
first is shaped as a Zipf curve of exponent $\alpha$: real
populations are concentrated, since an up-to-date browser on a given platform
emits essentially one JA4~\cite{althouse2023ja4}. We
calibrated that curve against $6.33$M TLS requests logged at a production edge
point of presence of Azion Technologies\footnote{Measured 22 August 2026 from an
access log the platform already collects. The extract holds only client-software
fingerprints and their frequencies, with no address, host, URI, header or
locale.}: $495$ distinct fingerprints, the most common accounting for $38.4\%$ of
requests and the top ten for $93.8\%$. The measured curve is bracketed by
$\alpha = 1.5$ and $\alpha = 2.0$. We take $\alpha = 1.5$ as canonical and sweep
the range. As for botnet composition, real botnets span device classes
such as routers, IP cameras and DVRs~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}, and therefore
TLS stacks, so we distribute $90\%$ of the campaign uniformly over $M$ stacks, the
conservative choice for a given $M$, and the rest over one-off fingerprints, and
take $M = 25$ as canonical. An \textit{adversarial} mode lets the botnet adopt
the most common benign fingerprints, as an impersonating bot would, and marks the
boundary of the threat model.
Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} reports the measurement and its caveats.

Legitimate distributions are calibrated against the $\sim$322k benign sessions of
CICIDS2017 and verified by Kolmogorov--Smirnov
tests: $D = 0.003$ for duration and $D = 0.002$ for request count, both with
$p > 0.8$, so the benign sessions are no easier than those of CICIDS2017. A \textit{stealth}
mode draws attacker sessions from the same distributions as benign traffic, so
the campaign reveals itself only through cross-session structure.

\textbf{Laboratory captures.} CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} (Wednesday capture:
Slowloris, Slowhttptest, HULK, GoldenEye) and
CIC-IoT2023~\cite{neto2023ciciot} (DDoS Slowloris and HTTP Flood), the latter being the dataset on which KLAGE
reports $F_1 = 84.1\%$ for \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, run through the same
extraction pipeline (PCAP $\rightarrow$ JA4 + flows $\rightarrow$ sessions
$\rightarrow$ graph).
\textbf{Production traffic.} Eight days of Azion's CDN traffic, as counts of
distinct origins, connections and same-/24 pairs per fingerprint and five-minute
window (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

\subsection{Baselines, Configurations and Metrics}

Each ablation configuration is run with $n = 30$ distinct seeds, giving bootstrap
intervals and paired tests. All configurations consume the same
traffic attributes, so the ablation separates \textit{representation} with data
availability held fixed. $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is fixed per scenario at the
99th percentile of $\Omega$ over legitimate clusters, and $\lambda_e$ so that the
scope names a filter in at most $1\%$ of them.

The per-session baseline is deliberately \textbf{strong}: a Random Forest (RF) over
all eight to nine available flow attributes (request count and duration, byte
and packet sums in both directions, mean and standard deviation of
inter-request time). To keep the negative result from depending on one
hypothesis class, we repeat it with histogram gradient boosting, a multilayer
perceptron and logistic regression over the same features. We also reimplement
three academic baselines: PCA profiling~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous}, PCA followed by $k$-means clustering over a behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca}, and supervised RF/SVM over session
features~\cite{kemp2023approach}. We compare against published KLAGE
numbers on the same dataset and attack. The four ablation configurations are:
\textbf{(a)} ML baseline without ontology; \textbf{(b)} ontology with \textit{no}
\texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation, sessions as isolated nodes; \textbf{(c)}
ontology with \textit{only} \texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity}, emulating a naive
ASN/prefix detector; and \textbf{(d)} the full cross-session representation over the three
sub-relations observable in the evaluated traffic. Thus (d)$-$(a) is the total contribution, (d)$-$(b) the
gain of representing cross-session structure, and (d)$-$(c) the gain
of high-weight signals over network proximity.

Configuration (d) consumes the cross-session evidence as \textit{features}: the
share of a cluster sharing one JA4 or /24, plus its size. This
feature-level proxy for the \texttt{relatedBy} family keeps every configuration
equal in data and classifier.

Beyond standard classification metrics we report \textbf{collateral damage}: the
fraction of legitimate traffic falling inside the scope derived from the verdict.
It is the false-positive rate of the filter an operator would deploy, which can
differ sharply from that of the detector (Section~\ref{sec:collateral}).
Evidence chains are assessed for \textit{completeness} (do they enumerate every
correlated session and activated sub-relation?) and \textit{actionability} (is the
derived scope coherent with the cluster discriminator?).

Every number so far came from a classifier, which says nothing about the
symbolic layer, so we evaluate the rule directly: the sessions matching the scope
of Section~\ref{sec:evidence} \textit{are} the flagged set, with no model training
and no label, and the decision arrives with its derivation. Since AUC ranks and
the rule decides, we force the classifier onto the rule's operating point
(Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}).

Against a monolithic or lightly fragmented botnet the learned model is
competitive at zero false positives ($91.7\%$ and $88.6\%$ recall against $89.8\%$
and $90.0\%$). At 25 stacks the rule pulls ahead, $90.3\%$ against $36.4\%$, a
lead it keeps over a model allowed $1\%$ FPR ($66.8\%$), although that model ranks
well (AUC $0.979$): its scores overlap benign traffic in the tail. That model reads
no profile of normal traffic, and the rule does. Given the profile as two
per-session features, the prevalence of the session's fingerprint and its
enrichment in the cluster, the model recovers $87.4\%$ at 25 stacks and $86.9\%$ at
one hundred, where stacks too small for a thousand-session profile to certify hold
the rule at $38.6\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:collateral}). It trains on labels of the
campaign it scores, which no operator holds during an attack; the rule matches it
up to 25 stacks with none. In the adversarial row the rule itself incurs a
$3.78\%$ false-positive rate, and the model reaches $23.4\%$ once allowed $1\%$.

Table~\ref{tab:symbolic} takes the cluster of largest $\Omega$ as fired, with no
threshold. Run per five-minute window over benign arrivals we spread across an
hour (Table~\ref{tab:perwindow}), $\Omega(S) \geq \tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ alone
fires on $78$--$86\%$ of attack windows and $0.6\%$ of clean ones, but also on
$80$--$100\%$ of flash crowds~\cite{jung2002flash}, and requiring the enrichment test to name a
fingerprint removes every false alarm and keeps the detection, with no collateral.

\textbf{Production traffic} (Table~\ref{tab:production}) breaks two assumptions
the generator builds in. One client opens many connections, so counted in
connections the scope names a filter in $60.1\%$ of clean windows, $26.9\%$ in
origins; and fleets of legitimate clients switch on together, so $\lambda_e$ is
calibrated on the preceding days, bringing the rule to $0.2\%$ of clean windows.
Its trigger acts as a volume threshold. A 99th-percentile threshold on distinct
origins fires on the same windows in $86$--$99.6\%$ of cases and, as the gate,
halves the false alarms with no loss of detection (6 against 14 clean windows).
The scope separates attack
from surge. On botnet stacks that real clients also present, enrichment blocks
$20.2\%$ and $61.6\%$ of 100 and 1\,000 attackers, where a filter of
unseen fingerprints blocks none, and it fires on $3.9\%$ of flash crowds of 100
users, where a $z$-score fires on $64.5\%$. Joined with the unseen filter behind
the origin gate, it fires on $0.1\%$ of clean windows and blocks $38.4\%$ and
$77.7\%$ of those botnets on new stacks, but its false alarms block a median
$29.9\%$ of the window's clients. We chose both refinements on these test days,
where they hold on each of the five; fresh days must confirm them
(Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

\subsection{Collateral Damage on Legitimate Traffic}
\label{sec:collateral}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig3_collateral.png}
\caption{Scope derivation: frequency versus enrichment ($\alpha = 1.5$, $n = 15$).}
\label{fig:collateral}
\end{figure}

Mitigation is only meaningful as a pair, what the derived scope blocks and what
it costs, which Fig.~\ref{fig:collateral} reports for the frequency and enrichment
rules over $n = 15$ campaigns each.

For a monolithic botnet the two agree: $89.8\%$ of attacker sessions blocked with
no collateral observed, where a global endpoint rate limit disconnects every
legitimate user; the frequency rule was validated in that regime, which is not
representative.
From five stacks on, the modal fingerprint of the cluster becomes a
\textit{legitimate} one and the rule inverts: $0.0\%$ of the attack blocked and
$39.0\%$ of legitimate traffic hit, a net loss. A more concentrated benign
population ($\alpha = 2.0$) raises that collateral to $61.1\%$.

Enrichment removes the failure, blocking $90.0\%$ and $90.3\%$ of the attack at
$M = 5$ and $25$ stacks with \textbf{no collateral observed} across the $n = 15$
campaigns, and reproducing the frequency rule where it worked. The surviving
$10\%$ is the tail of attackers with one-off fingerprints, so scoped mitigation
trades completeness for precision. At $M = 100$ coverage falls to $38.6\%$, still
with no collateral: a stack of about ten sessions in two thousand cannot be told
from the tail of a $1\,000$-session profile, and pooling 30 attack-free periods
into the profile restores $89.6\%$.

\textbf{Two conditions bound the result.} The first is the boundary of the
threat model: when the botnet adopts common benign fingerprints, little is
enriched and the rule blocks $30.4\%$ of the attack at $3.78\%$ collateral,
against $3.6\%$ and $39.0\%$ for the frequency rule, and with a stricter $\rho$
the scope declines to name a fingerprint, the correct report when no
discriminator exists. The second is the background profile. Moderate drift is
tolerable ($\alpha = 2.0$ profile against an $\alpha = 1.5$ episode: $90.3\%$
coverage, no collateral), while a flat or missing profile makes every fingerprint
look rare and collateral jumps to $77.6\%$. A fresh and ample profile is thus a
deployment requirement: where it fails a freshness or drift check, scoped
mitigation is suppressed and the verdict emitted as evidence only.

\subsection{Cost and the Choice of $W$}
\label{sec:latency}

Kept as classes (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}), the equality-based sub-relations make admission constant at about
$0.37\,\mu$s per session and the symbolic layer linear, at $26.4$~s for
$100\,000$ sessions against $52.8$~s for pair edges at $1\,000$: $\Omega(S)$ grows
with the square of the cluster, computing it no longer does. The AUC of (d) is
flat over a $30\times$ range of $W$; the rule ran at $W = 300$~s.

\subsection{Statistical Significance}

Paired Wilcoxon tests over the $n = 30$ runs, Bonferroni-corrected over four
comparisons, make \textbf{(d)$-$(c)} and \textbf{(d)$-$(a)} significant at
$K = 1000$ ($p_{\mathrm{Bonf}} = 7.5\times 10^{-9}$, Cohen's $d = 13.5$ and
$22.4$). Seeds come from one generator, so the tests show a stable difference and
predict nothing about the field.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

We discuss partitioning, then what bounds the results and what the graph adds
(limitations continue in Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

\textbf{Parallelism and partitioning.} Condition (ii), one endpoint per $S$, makes
the endpoint the natural shard key: partitioning by it evaluates the rule
\textit{exactly}, with no cross-shard traffic and per-shard state bounded by the window. A campaign spreading one JA4 across many endpoints then accumulates too few
pairs in any shard to clear $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$, while sharding by JA4 breaks
the endpoint condition. No single key preserves all six sub-relations, and
Appendix~\ref{app:cost} sketches a two-tier design.

\textbf{The gain is regime-specific.} Per-IP thresholds handle single-source
floods and a strong per-session classifier reaches $\mathrm{AUC} \geq 0.98$ on
public-dataset attacks; only distributed \textit{and} stealthy campaigns need the
framework.

\textbf{Each evaluation establishes one thing.} The generator shows the
mechanism under a mimicry imposed by construction, attacker sessions drawing their
attributes from real benign distributions, so the non-separability of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} is no artifact of a weakened baseline. Production traffic
measures false alarms on real clients, and detection only for an injected botnet,
over eight days of one operator. Where its traffic matches the threat model (E1,
a botnet a tenth of the typical window), the calibrated level holds enrichment to
$0.7\%$ of the attackers on two held-out days, and a profile that knows the
endpoint's fleets lifts it to $6.3\%$, near the unseen filter ($6.5\%$) and the
$z$-score ($7.3\%$); an attacker presenting a known fleet's fingerprint escapes it.
No evaluation covers a captured stealthy campaign.

\textbf{The representation and the scope depend on an observable high-weight
discriminator.} As the fraction of origins sharing the TLS fingerprint falls,
$\mathrm{AUC}$(d) decreases monotonically from $1.00$ to $\approx 0.74$ under
randomization (a sweep of $n = 10$ in the artifact), and endpoint convergence
cannot compensate, since legitimate users converge there too. The volume trigger
survives the loss of JA4, but the scope then finds no discriminator. Jittered
timing and rotated identities leave the scope intact, since it rests on JA4; a bot outside the threat model that also presents a browser's JA4
defeats it, and Encrypted Client Hello~\cite{ietf2026ech} could hide JA4.

\textbf{What the graph adds.} Configuration (d) expresses the graph's evidence
as features, and a linear model over the same three cross-session features still
attains $0.799$ against $0.495$ for the per-session set, so the AUC gain of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} comes from representing cross-session structure. OWL adds
nothing to that number, nor to the trigger, which at window scale is volume, and
a learned model that reads the rule's profile and the campaign's labels matches or
exceeds the rule (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}). What the graph adds is operational,
and measurable. The ontology marks what each equality sub-relation equates, its
weight, and the unit classes are counted in, and the query that counts the rule's
classes is compiled from it. On generated sessions the compiled SQL reproduces
$\Omega$ exactly; run in the operator's log store for one day, it returns the
exported origin and /24-pair counts in all 1\,152 windows, and the JA4 class sizes
wherever the WAF blocked no client, although the eight-day evaluation read the
hand-written export. Given a binding of properties to columns, an equality signal
added to the ontology as four triples reaches the query with no change to any
code. The verdict, its decomposition and the scope (Listing~\ref{lst:chain})
export as STIX~2.1 bundles that pass the OASIS validator in strict mode and cross
the OASIS reference TAXII~2.1 server unchanged, save the extension definition it
fails to serve. MISP's importer keeps the \textit{course-of-action} and the
endpoint but drops the fingerprints, which STIX~2.1 can carry only in an
extension, so a MISP-fed playbook would scope the whole endpoint: JA4 needs a
standard property. The verdict is
\textit{explainable} in this sense of an inspectable derivation; whether it
shortens an analyst's decision is untested.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We modeled the HTTP session as a first-class entity, related to other sessions by
six typed sub-properties weighted by evasion cost, and fired a SPARQL/SWRL rule
whose derivation serves as verdict, evidence chain and mitigation scope.

Against generated stealthy campaigns of compromised devices, per-session
detection sits at chance while cross-session features reach ROC AUC
$0.93$--$0.98$, and on conventional laboratory attacks a strong
per-session classifier suffices. Scoping by the cluster's most common property
selects a legitimate fingerprint once the botnet spans several TLS stacks,
whereas a binomial enrichment test over normal traffic restores $90\%$ coverage
at 25 stacks with no observed collateral and no label, and fires on no generated
flash crowd; a learned model matches or exceeds it only with the same profile
and the campaign's labels. On CDN traffic the trigger reduces to a count of
distinct origins, and the calibrated test still names stacks shared with real
clients while ignoring most flash crowds, though where the threat model holds it
certifies few, a case a profile that knows the endpoint's fleets lifts from
$0.7\%$ to $6.3\%$ on held-out days; richer fleet models, HTTP/2 attacks and a captured stealthy
campaign come next.

\section{Runtime Construction and Sub-Relation Instantiation}
\label{app:algorithms}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Runtime knowledge-graph construction}
\label{alg:kg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE request stream $\mathcal{R}$, ontology $\mathcal{O}$, window $W$
\ENSURE knowledge graph $KG$ persistent over $W$
\STATE $KG \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FOR{each request $r \in \mathcal{R}$}
  \STATE $s,i,e \leftarrow \textsc{ResolveSession/Identity/Endpoint}(r)$
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{add}(s,i,e)$; \, $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{hasIdentity},i)$
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{targets},e)$
  \FOR{each active $s' \in S_W$, $s' \neq s$}
    \FOR{each sub-relation $i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}$}
      \IF{$\textsc{Holds}_i(s,s')$}
        \STATE $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{relatedBy}_i,s')$
      \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{purgeOutside}(W)$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $KG$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:kg} constructs the graph over the sliding window. It enumerates
pairs for clarity: the equality-based relations are kept as class counts
(Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}), and for the others the following procedures decide,
for each candidate pair $(s_a,s_b)$, whether the edge is instantiated.

\textbf{\texttt{TLSFingerprint}.} Two-stage match: exact equality of the full
JA4 or, failing that, near variants (same transport/TLS-version/ALPN
prefix, at most one symbol of difference in the ordered cipher or extension
blocks), instantiated with a \textit{ja4\_distance = 1} annotation. This absorbs
version drift within one client library without admitting spurious matches
across distinct stacks. Cost $O(1)$ per pair.

\textbf{\texttt{ReusedIdentity}.} Instantiated on any shared cookie, token or
username, with JWTs compared by their \texttt{sub} claim and cookies and usernames
normalized (trim, lower-case). Cost $O(1)$ amortized with an inverted index.

\textbf{\texttt{TemporalPattern}.} With $\Delta(s)$ the inter-arrival times of a
session of at least three requests, the edge holds when
$\mathrm{DTW}(\Delta(s_a),\Delta(s_b)) / \max(|\Delta(s_a)|,|\Delta(s_b)|) \leq
\tau_{\mathrm{DTW}}$. FastDTW under a Sakoe--Chiba band makes the cost linear, and
hashing summary vectors avoids all-pairs comparison.

\textbf{\texttt{PayloadSignature}.} Over the mean and deviation of body size and
hashes of the dominant User-Agent and Content-Type, the edge holds when cosine
similarity exceeds $\tau_{\mathrm{payload}}$.

\textbf{\texttt{EndpointConvergence}.} Instantiated when both sessions converge
on the same endpoint, under path-pattern normalization
(\texttt{/api/users/12345} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{/api/users/\{id\}}) by default;
an additional exact-path match annotates the edge with \textit{exact = true}. Cost
$O(1)$ with a prefix-tree index.

\textbf{\texttt{NetworkProximity}.} Instantiated on a shared IPv4 /24 (or IPv6
/48) prefix, or a shared ASN, or both; when both hold the edge is annotated
\textit{strong = true}. ASN resolution uses current BGP tables. Cost $O(1)$ with
an inverted prefix index.

\section{Weight Calibration}
\label{app:calibration}

Calibration depends on the realism of the traffic. On
laboratory datasets, with a few dozen distinct JA4 fingerprints, optimization
even inverts the sign of the TLS signal, because benign sessions share
fingerprints more than partially coordinated campaigns do, a testbed artifact of
low JA4 diversity.

In the realistic scenario users hit the attacked service with fingerprints drawn
from a pool of $2\,000$, whose Zipf shape the $495$ measured at one point of
presence fix; the pool exceeds the $76$--$818$ that each production endpoint of
Appendix~\ref{app:window} shows over its calibration days, so the benign tail runs
longer than observed. We calibrate by maximizing per-session
attack-vs-benign ROC AUC over the score
$w_{\mathrm{tls}}\,z(\mathrm{ja4}) + w_{\mathrm{ep}}\,z(\mathrm{endpoint\ conv.})
+ w_{\mathrm{net}}\,z(\mathrm{net\ prox.})$ with standardized attributes, over
the grid $\{0.3\ldots1.0\}^3$ (125 combinations, 60 scenarios, 91\,500
sessions). The result corroborates the proposed ordering: the best vector is
$(w_{\mathrm{tls}}{=}1.0,\ w_{\mathrm{ep}}{=}0.3,\ w_{\mathrm{net}}{=}0.3)$, and
the TLS fingerprint is the only individually discriminative signal (isolated
$\mathrm{AUC} = 0.93$, against $0.50$ for endpoint convergence and $0.58$ for
network proximity). The weights of Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology}
$(1.0,\ 0.6,\ 0.3)$ attain the same optimal $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.943$ and are
insensitive to $\pm20\%$ perturbation. Calibration
therefore validates the \textit{ordering}; in the pure same-service regime the
medium and low weights are not separately identifiable, since both
sit near chance. Their absolute values remain open: the per-window rule detects
the same with uniform weights (Appendix~\ref{app:window}), so they order the
evidence more than they drive detection.

\subsection*{Generator Realism}

The measurement is weighted per request, which if anything \textit{overstates}
concentration, and comes from one point of presence over one window; its head
matches $\alpha = 1.5$ and its top ten $\alpha = 2.0$, so the sweep brackets it.

\section{Additional Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\textbf{Session and identity instrumentation.} The approach assumes sessions,
identities and TLS fingerprints can be extracted from traffic events; partial or
noisy instrumentation degrades it. Counting origins by
source address undercounts users behind a CGN address and overcounts clients
that rotate IPv6 privacy addresses, whose natural unit is the /64.

\textbf{Graph maintenance cost.} The symbolic figures of
Appendix~\ref{app:cost} are an upper bound, for the reason given there, and
the production run of Appendix~\ref{app:window} is offline, over aggregated
counts per endpoint, so a live CDN deployment still needs the partitioning of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, which we did not implement.

\textbf{Protocol coverage.} HTTP/2 attacks and other application protocols (DNS,
gRPC) need sub-relations beyond the Slow HTTP DoS family over HTTP/1.1.

\textbf{Sub-relation coverage.} $\Omega(S)$ instantiates the three sub-relations
the generator and captures expose; identity reuse, temporal
pattern and payload signature are specified (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}) but
not exercised, so their weights are untested.

\textbf{Dataset breadth.} Laboratory validation covers one dataset and one attack
for the head-to-head comparison, plus six attacks across CICIDS2017 and
CIC-IoT2023 for the regime analysis; RT-IoT2022, CIC-DDoS2019 and a captured
stealthy campaign remain future work. Newer benchmarks keep the gap: inadequate
application-layer representation is named a deficiency of
BCCC-cPacket-Cloud-DDoS-2024~\cite{shafi2024bccc}, whose 17 DDoS scenarios are all
TCP-based, and we found no independent capture of the stealthy Layer-7 regime.

\textbf{Explanation validation.} The evidence chains of the seven laboratory
clusters pass completeness and actionability checks ($7/7$) when counted
in sessions; counted in origins, the five of CICIDS2017 hold one to three
sources, below $k_{\min}$, and the two of CIC-IoT2023 hold seven; no user study
tested them.

\section{Cost Model and Window Sensitivity}
\label{app:cost}

\begin{figure}[tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig5_latency.png}
\caption{Cost of both layers, with pair edges and with class counts.}
\label{fig:latency}
\end{figure}

Admission happens per request while materialization and the $\Omega$
aggregation run once per window, so we time the two layers separately, on a
synthetic window in the realistic mix (30\% of sessions in one campaign sharing a
botnet JA4, benign JA4 from a pool of 2\,000, eight endpoints, dispersed /24s),
with medians over three repeats on one core. Each layer runs in two ways: with
pair edges, as the rules are written, and with class counts for the
equality-based sub-relations. Both give the same $\Omega(S)$ for every endpoint,
to within $7\times 10^{-12}$. This is a cost model: the symbolic layer runs on
\texttt{rdflib} in place of Jena/TDB2, so its figures are
an upper bound, and counting origins adds one hash-set lookup per admission.

With pair enumeration, median admission latency grows from
$2.3\,\mu$s at $|S_W| = 100$ to $127\,\mu$s at $|S_W| = 10\,000$,
tracking the candidate-pair count (148 to 6\,495 edges per admission) at
$15$--$21$~ns per pair. With class counters it stays between
$0.33$ and $0.42\,\mu$s from $100$ to $100\,000$ sessions
(Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}, where dotted lines are reference slopes).

Materializing pair edges costs $0.84$~s at $|S_W| = 100$ and
$52.8$~s at $1\,000$, within $198$--$309\,\mu$s per RDF edge,
so that path is linear in edges and quadratic in sessions. Class aggregation
materializes one membership triple per session and sub-relation, and computes
$\Omega(S)$ with the query of Listing~\ref{lst:rules} in $0.33$~s at $1\,000$
sessions, $161$ times faster, and $26.4$~s at $100\,000$. The
quadratic term thus lives in the value of $\Omega(S)$, which counts pairs, and in
the pair edges of the non-transitive sub-relations, whose candidates the indexes
restrict. The evidence chain needs only the pair counts
(Listing~\ref{lst:chain}), which the classes give.

$W$ enters only through detection-cluster assignment, so a sweep over
$W = 60$--$1800$~s recomputes the cross-session features on the same scenarios
($K = 1000$, $n = 10$): the AUC of (d) stays at $0.976$--$0.978$ and that of (c)
at $0.663$--$0.664$, since the discriminative feature is the \textit{fraction} of
a cluster sharing one JA4. The scenarios target one endpoint, and $W$ must let a
cluster form ($\approx 132$ sessions at $W = 60$~s).

\subsection*{Partitioning at CDN Scale}

The design that endpoint sharding suggests, unimplemented here, is two-tiered:
endpoint-sharded workers plus a global sketch over high-weight identifiers to
recover campaigns that spread across endpoints. Quantifying the recall lost by
the first tier and recovered by the second is a precondition for CDN-scale
deployment. With class counting both layers stay linear in the window and
admission well under a microsecond; confirming it at CDN scale is future work.

\section{The Rule per Window}
\label{app:window}

threshold sees an attack or a label. $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is the 99th
percentile of $\Omega$ over the windows of 30 attack-free runs, which also form
the profile; false alarms are counted on 30 more, detection on the canonical
campaigns ($\alpha = 1.5$, $M = 25$) over windows with at least $k_{\min} = 5$
attackers, and a flash crowd retimes 25, 50 or 100 legitimate sessions of a third
run into one window. We spread benign starts over 60~min, since the generator

\textbf{Production traffic.} The same rule ran on eight days of access logs from
the four endpoints of the operator's own services whose windows hold at least
$k_{\min}$ origins. Per fingerprint and window, the exports carry the distinct
client addresses, connections and same-/24 pairs, counted inside the log store so
that no address leaves it. Each of the last five days is tested
with the profile, thresholds and $\lambda_e$ of the days before it, clean traffic
excludes clients the WAF blocked, and the canonical botnet is injected into the
counts (Table~\ref{tab:production}). The exports carry no prefixes, so the scope
is judged on JA4 alone.

Fleets, such as a probe fleet active only in business hours, push $\lambda_e$ to
about $10^{-4}$ (E4), $10^{-14}$ (E3), $10^{-60}$ (E1) and $10^{-73}$ (E2), and
the scope alone then names a filter in $1.5$--$6.3\%$ of the next day's windows.
When the rule fires on a clean window of E2 or E3 it names a fleet, blocking about
half of that window's clients, and up to $70\%$ in an attack window where it names
a fleet with the botnet. The origin gate halves these false alarms: the eight
clean windows that only $\Omega$ admits, with the scope firing, carry $33\%$ of
their $\Omega$ outside the endpoint term, against $12\%$ elsewhere, since a fleet
concentrates on one fingerprint and lifts the TLS term where the scope misfires.

Two limits bind detection. On E1 at 1\,000 attackers and on E4 at the typical
window's size the scope names botnet stacks in most windows, but $\Omega$ falls
below $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ in $57\%$ and $94\%$ of them; on E2 and E3 the
stacks of a botnet that size hold one or two origins, which no calibrated level
certifies. At a tenth of the typical window only E1 holds more than 100
attackers, and there the calibrated level stops enrichment at $0.6\%$, where the
unseen filter stops $11.2\%$ and the $z$-score, which fires on $5.1\%$ of E1's
clean windows, $12.5\%$;
the union recovers the unseen filter's share, and on shared stacks only the
$z$-score stops any ($12.2\%$).

A profile that knows the endpoint's fleets narrows this limit. Fingerprints the
test names at the nominal level in at least $5\%$ of an endpoint's calibration
windows become known fleets, which leave the scope and the level's calibration.
E1 has five or six of them, $7\%$ of the profile, and removing them raises its
level from about $10^{-60}$ to $10^{-21}$. Of the four shares tried on the first
three test days, $5\%$ was the only one that did not raise false alarms. On the
last two it lifts enrichment on E1 at a tenth of the typical window from $0.7\%$
to $6.3\%$ of the attackers ($0.2\%$ to $4.4\%$ on shared stacks, which the unseen
filter never stops), at 7 false alarms against 8, and leaves E2 to E4 unchanged.
On those days smaller shares block more but fire twice as often, on lighter filters
(a median $11$--$12\%$ of clients against $51\%$) and on more flash crowds, so
by collateral the chosen share is not the best one. A profile per hour of day did not help, and $\rho = 2$ or $5$ moves no
pooled firing rate of the table by more than $0.3$ points, no blocked share by
more than $4.5$, and a collateral median by up to $7.2$. Flash crowds of 1\,000 users
trigger the rule in $21.8\%$ of windows and the $z$-score in $86.8\%$. On the full
traffic of E3 and E4, $84\%$ and $96\%$ of the clients the scope would block were
also blocked by the WAF, against $1.5\%$ on E2, whose filters name fleets; E1 has
no WAF activity.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The reference implementation is available at
\url{https://github.com/Natanael0liveira/knowledge-graph-ddos-article} under a
permissive license, organized by stage: extraction (PCAP $\rightarrow$ JA4 and
flows $\rightarrow$ sessions $\rightarrow$ graph), the calibrated generator, the
ablation and baselines, the symbolic layer, and the evidence and mitigation
modules.
The count query is compiled from the ontology and checked on generated sessions,
and the four exported bundles pass the OASIS STIX~2.1 validator in strict mode
and are ingested over TAXII~2.1 and by MISP's importer (\texttt{make compile-check
stix-ingest}). Both scope derivations ship side by side, so the negative result of
Section~\ref{sec:collateral} is reproducible rather than asserted. Profiles and
levels come from attack-free runs, and labels enter only the evaluation: the
scripts behind Table~\ref{tab:symbolic} pick the largest-$\Omega$ cluster with no
label, and the per-window evaluation uses none before scoring.

Seeds are fixed and Makefiles drive every stage, so each table and data figure
regenerates from one command, except the inputs of Table~\ref{tab:production},
the operator's aggregates, whose anonymized summary ships with the artifact and is
checked against the table. The source of Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology} ships too, with
the OWL ontology in RDF/XML and every \texttt{relatedBy} sub-property annotated
with its \texttt{coordinationWeight}. We intend to submit the artifacts to the
Artifact Evaluation track.
