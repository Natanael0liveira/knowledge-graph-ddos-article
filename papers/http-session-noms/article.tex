\title{TLS-Fingerprint Scoping of Application-Layer DDoS Mitigation:\\Calibration Floors and Collateral on a CDN Operator's Endpoints}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Natanael Oliveira, Arthur Kobielski, Marcos Luna,
Luigi Carro, J\'eferson Campos Nobre, and Eder John Scheid}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Institute of Informatics, Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS), Porto Alegre, Brazil\\
\texttt{\{natanael.oliveira, arthur.kobielski, marcos.luna, carro, jcnobre, ejscheid\}@inf.ufrgs.br}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A low-rate distributed application-layer flood keeps every source below
per-source thresholds, so the decision is which clients to block. A binomial test,
calibrated on attack-free windows, names the TLS fingerprints over-represented among
an alarm's origins against the endpoint's normal-traffic profile. On generated
traffic the alarm's most common fingerprint blocks no attacker and $39\%$ of
legitimate traffic from five TLS stacks on, while profile-relative scopes block
$90\%$ of a botnet on up to 25 stacks absent from legitimate traffic, without
observed collateral. On a CDN's four endpoints, the binomial configuration misfires
on $0.1\%$ of clean windows of the days it was chosen on, blocking a median third of
clients. A held-out day, whose gate barely opened, is consistent but weakly
informative. Client fleets raise a calibration floor: a 25-stack botnet on rare
fingerprints real clients share is named only past $8\%$ of the busiest endpoint's
window and 4 to 19 median windows elsewhere, and in expectation never on the 4 to 28
most common. On the busiest endpoint the distinct-origin gate lets the configuration
stop only $12\%$ of a botnet a tenth the window's size. Post hoc, the
unseen-fingerprint filter as its own trigger stops $90\%$ of it there on new stacks
within budget but none on shared stacks, where a cross-fitted beta-binomial behind a
seasonal gate stops $15\%$ ($20\%$ on new ones). The operator's WAF verdicts cannot
serve as labels. An OWL ontology specifies the counts and the scope, a JA4 filter no
examined standard expresses in its core vocabulary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Application-layer Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks span two extremes. At one end, HTTP/2 Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487) pushed mitigation peaks to 398 million requests per second~\cite{google2023rapidreset}, a volume that capacity and scrubbing absorb. At the other end sit low-rate distributed floods, the target of this paper. \textit{Distributed Slow HTTP DoS} and rate-based variants that still hold connections open, such as HULK and GoldenEye~\cite{cambiaso2013slowdos}, exhaust a server from many coordinated origins, each sending too little to cross any per-source threshold.

A stealthy campaign also makes each session look legitimate on the per-session signals that detectors use, such as connection duration and route entropy~\cite{kemp2023approach}. What still sets it apart lies in the structure \textit{between} sessions: the same client fingerprint recurring across many origins that converge on one endpoint. Acting on it is a management decision: \textit{which} clients to block, on \textit{what} evidence, with a filter narrow enough to spare the service's legitimate users. The operator also needs to know, before an attack, where no such filter can be built, so that it can fall back there to a rate limit or a challenge.

Existing work detects the attack and leaves that decision open. Of 75 application-layer DDoS detectors in one survey, $47\%$ take their features from the request stream of a user session and its packet pattern~\cite{odusami2020survey}. Graph-based detectors keep relations between network nodes and stop at a report~\cite{belcastro2025klage}. Aggregate-based mitigations filter on network and transport headers~\cite{mahajan2002pushback,alcoz2022accturbo}, source-based filtering weighs each blocked prefix against the legitimate traffic it carries~\cite{soldo2012filtering}, and the standard channels that carry a mitigation request filter on the same fields~\cite{dots2020data,rfc8955flowspec}. No published method blocks by application-client fingerprint while counting what each such filter costs in legitimate clients and saying where it can no longer be built.

This paper turns that decision into a calibrated statistical test (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). For each endpoint and five-minute window, a volume trigger raises the alarm. The \textit{scope}, the set of fingerprints to block, holds the TLS fingerprints over-represented among the alarm's origins relative to a \textit{profile} of the endpoint's normal traffic, at a level set on attack-free windows. We target botnets of compromised devices, whose TLS stacks their operator cannot cheaply replace, and we evaluate bots that present common fingerprints as the method's boundary. The paper makes three contributions. \1~On generated traffic we show when the natural scope, the alarm's most common fingerprint, blocks users and no attackers, and that scopes relative to the profile block the attack without labels, including stacks that real clients share, where they block $2.2$--$3.4\%$ of legitimate traffic. \2~On eight days of four CDN endpoints we measure what calibration costs: the false alarms, the \textit{calibration floor}, the botnet size at which the scope names a typical stack, and a harder enrichment-ratio limit, past which it is expected to name none. We also measure how much of an injected botnet the trigger lets through, compare post hoc triggers that recover part of it, and analyze a held-out day as fixed in advance. \3~No standard we examined expresses the resulting scope, a filter over JA4 fingerprints, in its core vocabulary. STIX~2.1 holds a JA4 only in a custom extension, OCSF records it but defines no filter~\cite{ocsf2025schema}, and DOTS and Flowspec match only network and transport fields. The counts the decision reads and the scope it exports are specified in an OWL ontology, from which the log store's query is compiled.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Table~\ref{tab:related} compares prior scopes and detectors on what a filter matches, how it is chosen and what it costs legitimate users.

\textbf{Scoping a mitigation.} Aggregate-based congestion control identifies the aggregate of an attack by header fields and rate-limits it upstream~\cite{mahajan2002pushback}, and a recent form scores aggregates in programmable switches~\cite{alcoz2022accturbo}. Blackholing drops all traffic to the attacked prefix, and advanced blackholing narrows it to header fields to limit that collateral damage~\cite{dietzel2018stellar}. Source-based filtering chooses which prefixes to block under a filter budget, trading the attack traffic stopped against the legitimate traffic of those prefixes~\cite{soldo2012filtering}. Closer to our test, feature distributions detect and classify traffic anomalies~\cite{lakhina2005mining}, and anomaly extraction isolates the feature values over-represented in anomalous traffic~\cite{silveira2010urca,brauckhoff2009extraction}, the question our test asks of fingerprints. Signature generation checks its candidates against a pool of normal traffic~\cite{li2006hamsa}. Against attacks that mimic flash crowds, defenses make every client solve a puzzle~\cite{kandula2005botz}, or schedule each session by how far it strays from legitimate workload profiles, weighing the delay imposed on legitimate users~\cite{ranjan2009ddosshield}. All of them work on network, flow, session or payload features, without the fingerprints of an application's clients. The IETF DOTS data channel installs filters on network and transport header fields~\cite{dots2020data}, as BGP Flow Specification does~\cite{rfc8955flowspec}, and neither can express an application-layer client fingerprint such as JA4.

\textbf{Layer-7 detection.} Layer-7 HTTP DDoS detectors~\cite{odusami2020survey} either profile traffic, applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to flow statistics~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous} or to a per-session behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca}, or learn from session features~\cite{kemp2023approach}. None of them keeps a relation between sessions. Flash crowds are told apart from floods by how requests spread over a site's documents~\cite{xie2009monitoring}.

\textbf{Knowledge graphs and fingerprints.} Security knowledge graphs link entities extracted from threat reports, or map countermeasures to ATT\&CK as D3FEND does in OWL~\cite{kaloroumakis2021d3fend}, and how to use them in operations remains unclear~\cite{liu2022recent}. Graphs built from traffic feed learned detectors: KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage}, the closest to our setting, classifies the nodes of graphs built from network logs. Passive TLS fingerprinting identifies client software from its \textit{ClientHello}~\cite{husak2016https}, and the many applications built on one library often share one fingerprint~\cite{anderson2019tls}. That sharing is what makes a stack usable as a scope, and also what limits it. Most bots that claim a browser's identity present the TLS and header fingerprints of another tool~\cite{li2021goodbot}. Industrial bot-management platforms aggregate signals per JA4 over recent traffic~\cite{martinetti2024ja4signals}, with no public model, benchmark or collateral metric.

\section{Calibrated Fingerprint Scoping}
\label{sec:approach}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1_scoping.png}
\caption{Calibrated fingerprint scoping. The query compiled from the session ontology returns, per endpoint and window, the class sizes the trigger and the scope read, and every threshold and level comes from attack-free days.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

This section describes the threat model, the trigger, the scope and its calibration, the floor that calibration sets, and what the ontology specifies (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

The defender operates the attacked application and observes, per request, the TLS
\textit{ClientHello}, the source address with its prefix and ASN, the target endpoint,
the arrival time, and whatever identity material the application exposes. From the
\textit{ClientHello} it computes a JA4 fingerprint~\cite{althouse2023ja4}, a string
that summarizes the TLS version, cipher suites, extensions and ALPN. The defender sees
neither the botnet's control channel nor which sources are coordinated, but can
profile the service's own traffic outside attack episodes. The attacker controls $K$
compromised devices and may make each session statistically indistinguishable from a
legitimate one, the stealth assumption of our evaluation. The harm is exhausted
capacity: each session holds a connection and a worker as a legitimate one does, so no
per-session rate rises. We also assume that a bot cannot easily change its TLS stack: a bot that speaks TLS does so through the library its device or toolkit ships, and botnets of compromised devices span many router, camera and DVR models~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}. Presenting a browser's \textit{ClientHello} takes an impersonation library kept in step with browser releases~\cite{frolov2019tls}. Bots that do so, such as headless-browser farms or bots built on TLS-impersonation libraries, fall outside this model, and Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates them as its boundary.

\subsection{The Trigger}
\label{sec:rule}

For each endpoint $e$ and five-minute window, let $S$ be the sessions to $e$ and $n$ their distinct origins, that is, source addresses. The trigger fires when $n \geq k_{\min} = 5$ and $n$ reaches its 99th percentile over attack-free windows. This \textbf{distinct-origin gate} is the trigger of the production evaluation. The base rule, kept by the protocol as a reference, uses instead the coordination mass $\Omega(S)$ of the ontology's rule \texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood}, with its threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ at the same percentile. $\Omega$ is a weighted count of the pairs of distinct origins whose sessions share a JA4, an endpoint or a /24 prefix (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}). It mostly measures volume: its endpoint term, $0.6\binom{n}{2}$, carries $91\%$ of it in the cluster of the default campaign (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}). What separates the attack from legitimate traffic is the scope.

\subsection{The Scope and Its Calibration}
\label{sec:evidence}

How the scope is chosen decides whether the mitigation helps or harms. The obvious
choice, the alarm's most common (modal) fingerprint, picks whatever is common, and on a
service under attack that is the legitimate population. A botnet of $A$ attackers
spread evenly over $M$ stacks puts about $0.9A/M$ on each and the remaining tenth on
one-off fingerprints, while the most common legitimate fingerprint holds a share $p_1$
of the $n_b$ legitimate origins. The modal fingerprint is an attack stack only while
$0.9A/M > p_1 n_b$. With the measured $p_1 = 38.4\%$ of requests
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), taken as a proxy for origins, and a campaign as large as the
legitimate traffic, this holds only while $M < 2.3$. With more stacks the filter blocks
users and no attackers.

We therefore compare the alarm with a \textbf{profile} of the endpoint's normal traffic, which the operator keeps outside attack episodes. Let $b(f)$ be the share of fingerprint $f$, plus $1/N$, among the profile's $N$ origin--fingerprint pairs, $c(f)$ the number of the alarm's origins that present $f$, and $n = \sum_f c(f)$. The \textbf{binomial enrichment test} names every $f$ that is both enriched, $c(f)/n \geq \rho\, b(f)$ with $\rho = 3$, and unlikely under the binomial background, $\Pr[X \geq c(f)] < \lambda_e/|F|$ for $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, b(f))$. The Bonferroni divisor $|F|$ counts the fingerprints of the profile and the alarm. The result is a \textit{set} of fingerprints, so it covers a botnet split over many stacks. A per-fingerprint \textbf{$z$-score} of $c(f)$ against $\mathrm{Bin}(n, b(f))$ gives a simpler scope relative to the profile. The \textbf{unseen} filter names the fingerprints absent from the profile and seen in at least $k_{\min}$ origins, and needs no profile statistics. Joined with the test (the \textbf{union}), it catches new stacks too small for the level.

On generated traffic each origin carries one session, and the level $\lambda_e$ stays at $0.01$. On production traffic, \textit{fleets} of legitimate clients switch on together and break the binomial's independence. We therefore set $\lambda_e$ per endpoint $e$ to an empirical quantile: the largest value up to $0.01$ at which the scope names a filter in at most $1\%$ of the endpoint's attack-free calibration windows (the \textit{budget}), about 2.9 of a day's 288. At levels such as $10^{-60}$ the binomial tail no longer gives the probability of the data. It works as a score, and the quantile sets its threshold. The $z$-score's threshold follows the same budget, as the 99th percentile of each calibration window's largest $z$, never below 3. Both budgets hold on the calibration windows by construction, and Section~\ref{sec:production} measures how well they hold on the days that follow.

The profile is what a defender sees in normal operation, so no attack label is involved. It must come from outside the attack episode, because the campaign spans the whole window and would otherwise give every candidate an enrichment of one. An attacker hiding inside a popular legitimate fingerprint, by contrast, is not enriched, and the test names no scope, the right answer when nothing tells the attack apart.

\subsection{The Calibration Floor}
\label{sec:floor}

The calibrated level has a price, the \textbf{calibration floor}. Among $n$ origins, the test names a stack of $c$ attackers only if $c/n \geq \rho\, b(f)$ and $\Pr[X \geq c] < \lambda_e/|F|$. The floor is the smallest botnet of $A$ attackers on $M$ stacks whose average stack passes both conditions, with $0.9A/M$ attackers in a window of $n_0 + A$ origins, $n_0$ being the median window of the calibration days. The lower the level, the higher the floor. It marks where the scope names a typical stack. In the union, the unseen filter names a stack absent from the profile once it holds $k_{\min}$ origins, which takes $\lceil k_{\min} M/0.9 \rceil$ attackers whatever the level, 139 for $M = 25$. The level's floor therefore matters on stacks that real clients also present, and there the ratio sets a harder limit. A stack's expected share of the window stays below $0.9/M$, so a botnet on $M$ stacks is not expected to enrich a fingerprint whose share exceeds $0.9/(\rho M)$, $1.2\%$ for $M = 25$ and $\rho = 3$.

We evaluate two ways to lower the floor. The first exempts the endpoint's \textit{known fleets}, the fingerprints named at the nominal level in at least $5\%$ of calibration windows, from the scope and from the calibration. The exemption is an allow-list, on the busiest endpoint of fingerprints that carry about $7\%$ of its profile, so the scope never blocks a bot that presents a fleet's fingerprint. The second, built after the held-out day was read (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}), is a \textbf{beta-binomial background}, which lets counts be correlated within a window. Its correlation $\phi(f)$ is a moment estimate per profile fingerprint over the calibration windows, from $\mathrm{Var}[c(f)] = n b(1-b)\bigl(1 + (n-1)\phi\bigr)$, clipped to $[0, 0.99]$, and each count is tested against a beta-binomial of the same mean. A fingerprint absent from the profile keeps the binomial.

\subsection{The Session Ontology as Specification}
\label{sec:ontology}
\label{sec:pipeline}

All the counts above are specified in the session ontology, which is what an operator would deploy. Its central class, \texttt{ApplicationSession}, links a session to its \texttt{Identity}, source \texttt{IPAddress}, \texttt{Endpoint} and \texttt{Mitigation}, whose scope the evidence chain carries. Sub-properties of a symmetric \texttt{relatedTo} type the evidence that links two sessions (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}). The three used here are equalities: the same JA4, the same endpoint and the same /24 prefix split the sessions into classes. The ontology annotates each with the session property it compares and \texttt{relatedTo} with the unit it counts in. From these annotations we compile the SQL query that returns, per window and endpoint, the class sizes that the gate, the base rule and the scope read, given only a binding of properties to columns. No reasoner runs in the evaluation. A new equality signal reaches the compiled query as four triples, plus a binding for any new session property, with no change to any code. When the rule fires, the evidence chain (the rule, the instances, the split of $\Omega(S)$ per sub-relation and the scope) is exported as JSON-LD (Listing~\ref{lst:chain}) and as STIX~2.1~\cite{oasis2021stix}, an \textit{indicator} and a \textit{course-of-action} linked by \textit{mitigates}.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

We use three kinds of traffic, each for one question. A generator, calibrated on benign CICIDS2017 sessions and on JA4 fingerprints measured at a CDN, runs the controlled campaigns. Laboratory captures test conventional attacks and allow an uncontrolled comparison with the published result of KLAGE, the closest system (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). Eight days of the CDN's traffic test the scopes' false alarms and, with a botnet injected, their detection.

\subsection{Scenarios and Datasets}

The main parameter of a campaign is its \textit{degree of distribution} $K$, the number of devices the attacker controls. We report $K = 50$, a small botnet whose origins each stay under their own rate limit, and $K = 1000$, spread over hundreds of ASNs and prefixes, with almost no per-session signal.

\textbf{Controlled evaluation: calibrated synthetic traffic.} A generator produces distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaigns against one endpoint, with known ground truth, and varies $K$, the sharing of TLS stacks and identities, the spread over IPs and ASNs, and the time window. Legitimate fingerprint popularity follows a Zipf curve of exponent $\alpha$, since real populations are concentrated. We fitted that curve to $6.33$M TLS requests logged at a production edge point of presence of Azion Technologies\footnote{Measured 22 August 2026 from an access log the platform already collects. The extract holds only client-software fingerprints and their frequencies, with no address, host, URI, header or locale.}: $495$ distinct fingerprints, the most common carrying $38.4\%$ of requests and the top ten $93.8\%$. The measured curve's head matches $\alpha = 1.5$ and its top ten $\alpha = 2.0$, and we take $\alpha = 1.5$ as the default. No published measurement fixes how botnets spread over stacks, so we vary it: $90\%$ of the campaign spreads evenly over $M$ stacks, the hardest case for a given $M$, and the rest over one-off fingerprints, with $M = 25$ as the default. A \textit{shared} mode draws the stacks from the profile's tail, fingerprints that real clients also present, and an \textit{adversarial} mode gives the botnet the most common legitimate fingerprints, as a bot that imitates a browser would.

Legitimate request counts and durations are sampled from the benign sessions of CICIDS2017 (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}), so Kolmogorov--Smirnov distances of $D = 0.002$ and $0.003$ between generated and real sessions only check the sampler. In \textit{stealth} mode each attacker session draws its attributes from the same distributions, so the campaign shows only in the structure across sessions. Attackers start within the first 30~s, which makes the campaign a large share of the traffic it overlaps: a median $34$--$45\%$ of a window's origins and $50\%$ of the alarm's cluster.

\textbf{Production traffic.} We use eight days of four endpoints of Azion's own services: real-user monitoring beacons (E1), a web console (E2), an API (E3) and single sign-on (E4), whose median windows hold about $3\,000$, 62, 41 and 20 origins. Of the 12 endpoints exported, they are the four that carry TLS and have at least 50 calibration windows and a median window of at least $k_{\min}$ origins. The exports count distinct origins and connections per fingerprint and five-minute window, and same-/24 pairs per window. The evaluation places the scope behind the operator's WAF, so the profiles and floors describe the clients the WAF lets through. We take the calibration days as attack-free: an attack among them would lower the level and raise the gate, which makes the scope more cautious. Test-day windows without an injected botnet (\textit{clean}) are taken as attack-free too, so a real attack there would count as a false alarm.

Each of the last five days is tested with the profile, thresholds and level of the days before it. Into each of its windows we inject a botnet built as the generator builds one. It holds 25 to 1\,000 attackers, or from a tenth of the median window (\textit{tenth-size}) to all of it. It puts $90\%$ of its attackers evenly on 25 stacks and the rest on one-off fingerprints that no scope names, so at most about $90\%$ can be blocked. Its stacks are absent from the traffic (\textit{new}), drawn from the profile past its ten most common fingerprints (\textit{shared}), or the endpoint's 25 most common fingerprints (\textit{adversarial}). A flash crowd adds 100 or 1\,000 users drawn from the day's own clients to one window. A ninth day, the \textit{held-out day}, was analyzed with the configuration fixed in advance.

\subsection{Baselines, Configurations and Metrics}
\label{sec:configs}

On generated traffic we compare four scopes on the alarm's cluster: the modal fingerprint, the unseen filter, the $z$-score ($z > 3$) and the binomial test. The learned baseline is a Random Forest trained on the campaign's labels over the cross-session evidence of the three sub-relations (configuration (d) of Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). It is held to zero false positives, the test's operating point on every row the learned model runs but the adversarial one. On production traffic we evaluate the configurations of Table~\ref{tab:configs}. Their gate is $\Omega \geq \tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ or distinct origins at their 99th percentile, and their thresholds, levels and known fleets are fitted on attack-free days. \textit{In sample}, each calibration day is judged against a profile that includes it. \textit{Cross-fitting}, built post hoc, judges it against a profile of the other days, as a test day is. Five choices were made on the test days: counting origins, calibrating $\lambda_e$, the gate, the union with the unseen filter and the $5\%$ fleet share. Of four shares, only $5\%$ had no more false alarms than the base rule on the first three test days, although $0.5\%$ blocked about 25 times fewer legitimate clients and more of a 100-attacker botnet (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). The values $\rho = 3$ and $k_{\min} = 5$, the 99th percentile of $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ and the 25-stack botnet were fixed on generated traffic before any production data were read.

Besides classification metrics we report \textbf{collateral damage}, the share of legitimate traffic inside the scope, which is the false-positive rate of the filter an operator would deploy. On production traffic each configuration has a false-alarm rate (FA), the share of clean windows where it names a filter (5\,643 on the test days, 1\,152 on the held-out day). Its exact 95\% interval assumes independent windows, although false alarms cluster by day. Each alarm also has a collateral (Coll.), the share of that window's clients the filter blocks, which the tables give as a median over alarms. The tables also give the rate of the scope without the gate (No gate, a diagnostic), the rate with a flash crowd of 1\,000 users (Fl.\,1k), and the share blocked of botnets of a median window or a tenth of it ($1\times$, $0.1\times$). The rate times the mean collateral is the expected share of legitimate clients blocked per clean window. The blocked share of an injected botnet is averaged over all windows, including those where the trigger does not fire. It is the product of how often the gate fires, how often the scope names the botnet when it does, and the share of attackers the named filter covers.

\section{Results}

We first show the mechanism on generated traffic. Production traffic then gives three findings: calibration keeps false alarms low but sets a floor, the trigger decides what is stopped, and the evidence beyond the test days is thin.

On generated traffic the sessions that match a scope \textit{are} the flagged set, with no training or label (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}), while per-session detection stays at chance (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

The natural scope fails. When the botnet uses a single stack, the modal fingerprint and the test agree, blocking $89.8\%$ of the attack with no collateral observed. From five stacks on, as Section~\ref{sec:evidence} predicts, the modal fingerprint is a \textit{legitimate} one: it blocks $0.0\%$ of the attack and $39.0\%$ of legitimate traffic, $61.1\%$ with a more concentrated legitimate population ($\alpha = 2.0$).

Profile-relative scopes succeed. The binomial test and the $z$-score block $90.0\%$ of the attack at five stacks and $90.3\%$ at 25, with \textbf{no collateral observed} across the campaigns. The rest sits on one-off fingerprints, so a scoped mitigation trades completeness for precision. At 100 stacks, a stack of about ten sessions among two thousand looks like the tail of a 1\,000-session profile, and the test falls well below the $z$-score and the unseen filter (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}). A profile pooled over 30 attack-free periods brings it back to $89.6\%$. The unseen filter, at a mean collateral of $0.03\%$ (at most $0.5\%$ in one seed), matches or beats the test because generated stacks never occur in legitimate traffic. It blocks none of the shared stacks, which real clients also present, and on those the test blocks $85.4\%$ at $2.23\%$ collateral and the $z$-score $89.9\%$ at $3.37\%$.

The learned model needs labels that no operator has during an attack, and at 25 stacks and zero false positives the test is well ahead of it. Given the profile as two per-session features, the model recovers most of the attack at 25 and 100 stacks (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}), but it is trained on labels of the very campaign it scores. Allowed $1\%$ false positives, which the test does not need, it reaches $66.8\%$ at 25 stacks, and $94.6\%$ with the profile.

\textbf{Two conditions bound the result.} When the botnet adopts common legitimate fingerprints, the boundary of the threat model, little is enriched: the test blocks $30.4\%$ of the attack at $3.78\%$ collateral, the $z$-score $57.3\%$ at $11.3\%$, the modal fingerprint $3.6\%$ at $39.0\%$ and the learned model $23.4\%$ at $1\%$. A flat or missing profile makes every fingerprint look rare, and collateral jumps to $77.6\%$, while moderate drift does little harm. Per window, $\Omega \geq \tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ alone also fires on flash crowds~\cite{jung2002flash}. The test removes those alarms for generated crowds (Appendix~\ref{app:window}), though not for crowds of production clients, as the next section shows.

\textbf{False alarms.} Tables~\ref{tab:production} and~\ref{tab:floor} give the results. Pooled over the endpoints, every calibrated component exceeds its $1\%$ target on the days after the calibration: the binomial configuration's scope alone names a filter in $2.2\%$ of clean test-day windows, and the distinct-origin gate alone fires in $3.0\%$, $5.4\%$ on the busiest endpoint. The configuration needs both, so it fires on only $0.1\%$ of clean windows (5 of 5\,643, 95\% interval $0.03$--$0.21\%$), 4 of them on the web console. A fleet can open the gate and enrich its own fingerprint at once, yet the 5 joint misfires, against 3.5 under independence, are within chance ($P = 0.27$), unlike those of the post hoc beta-binomial and the $z$-score (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). These misfires block a median $32.9\%$ of the window's clients, about $0.03\%$ of legitimate clients per clean window. Crowds of 1\,000 of the day's clients trigger the configuration in $22.2\%$ of windows, with lighter filters that block a median $4.3\%$ of the window's clients.

\textbf{What the scope can name: the calibration floor.} The strict level that keeps false alarms rare also sets the floor. On the busiest endpoint fleets push $\lambda_e$ down to $10^{-60}$, and exempting its five or six known fleets raises it to about $10^{-21}$. With the unseen filter, the binomial configuration names a 25-stack botnet on new stacks from 139 attackers per window on E1 to E3, a bound set by $k_{\min}$, and from 84 on E4, where the test names them first (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). On stacks that real clients also present, mostly rare ones past the profile's ten most common fingerprints, the level decides, and the floor is computed at their median share. A typical stack is then named only past 254 attackers on the busiest endpoint, $8\%$ of its window, and past 4 to 19 times the median window on the small ones, as medians of the daily ratios. The floor grows with the stacks, from $2\%$ of the busiest window at 5 stacks to $34\%$ at 100, and with the fingerprints' rank: those of rank 11 to 35 are never named on the two largest endpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). It is no cliff: below it the scope names the stacks that chance makes larger, as a binomial model of stack sizes predicts ($43.1\%$ against $43.2\%$ measured at 100 attackers on the busiest endpoint, Fig.~\ref{fig:stops}).

\textbf{Past the ratio limit the scope is expected to name nothing.} The 4 to 28 most common fingerprints of each endpoint, which carry $86$--$94\%$ of its origins, lie past that limit for a 25-stack botnet. A botnet on the endpoint's 25 most common fingerprints, the boundary of the threat model, is mostly missed: the configuration stops $7.0\%$ and $23.8\%$ of 100 and 1\,000 attackers. It stops none on the two largest endpoints, where nearly all 25 lie past the limit, but $73.5\%$ of 1\,000 on the single sign-on, where only 4 or 5 do.

\subsection{What the Evidence Supports}
\label{sec:posthoc}
\label{sec:boundary}

\textbf{The held-out day.} Exported after the configuration was fixed and analyzed with metrics fixed in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), the held-out day gives the binomial configuration 2 false alarms in 1\,152 clean windows ($P = 0.27$, and up to 3 would have passed). This test is weak. Beyond the protocol's metrics, a tripled rate would be flagged only $37\%$ of the time, and the day passed mostly because the gate was quiet: it opened in only 3 clean windows, $0.26\%$ against $3.0\%$ on the test days. The scope misfired in two of them, both on the web console, more often than the test days predict once the gate is open ($P = 0.003$). Flash crowds of 100 users trigger the configuration in $1.1\%$ of windows, and at a tenth of the busiest window the gate never opened. On the single sign-on, crowds of 1\,000 users, also beyond the protocol, trigger it in $97.2\%$ of windows against $34.1\%$ on the test days, nearly all on one fingerprint more common that day (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

\textbf{Post hoc analyses.} The beta-binomial and a cross-fitted calibration were built after the held-out day was read, so their results are post hoc. In sample the beta-binomial lowers the shared-stack floor on the three largest endpoints (Table~\ref{tab:floor}), but 19 of its 22 false alarms fall on the web console, where $74.4\%$ of 1\,000-user crowds trigger it. On the held-out day it raised no false alarm. A first design of the cross-fitting, which calibrated on the last calibration day alone, was dropped after its results were seen, since one day misses the rare fleet events that set the level. Under cross-fitting the binomial configuration barely moves (Fig.~\ref{fig:operating}), with 6 false alarms against 5. The calibrated $z$-score's lead in detection came from its in-sample threshold, which cross-fitting raises by a median factor of 1.8. Only the beta-binomial nearly meets its target out of sample: its scope alone fires in $1.1\%$ of clean windows, and the console's 1\,000-user crowds trigger it in $16.1\%$ of windows. Its floor on the rare shared stacks of Table~\ref{tab:floor} is below the binomial's cross-fitted floor on every endpoint, though not everywhere on more common stacks against the in-sample binomial (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

\textbf{This WAF's verdicts cannot serve as labels.} The operator's WAF, the only source of labels at hand, blocked a median $9\%$, $63\%$ and $39\%$ of each window's clients on E2 to E4, and none on E1. With a profile built without those clients, the scopes' agreement with the WAF is built in: a stable fingerprint is enriched there only if $70$--$88\%$ of its clients were blocked. With a profile of all clients, only $0.4$--$33\%$ of the clients the three profile-relative scopes would block were blocked, less than a random pick ($10$--$63\%$) on every endpoint. On the API and single sign-on the scopes catch at most 2 of the WAF's 38 surges (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Which configuration to recommend.} The floor and the trigger decide per endpoint. On the three small endpoints the binomial configuration names shared stacks only past 4 to 19 windows and new stacks from 2 to 4 windows, so for smaller botnets the fallback is a rate limit or a challenge to every client. \1~\textit{Now} we would deploy the binomial configuration on every endpoint, with the fallback below its floor, because behind the distinct-origin gate it alone was tested on a day it had not seen, as fixed in advance. A botnet that leaves the gate shut meets neither filter nor fallback on any endpoint, so on the busiest one the configuration stops few small botnets, none on the held-out day. On the console, where both held-out false alarms fell, a challenge may be the safer first response. \2~\textit{Next}, a test pre-specified on new days over a weekly cycle with an external timestamp would compare post hoc triggers on 100 attackers and on a tenth of the window. Its baseline, the unseen filter as its own trigger, matched every scope of Table~\ref{tab:triggers} on new stacks of at least $k_{\min}$ origins within the budget everywhere, pooled over the days, with $90\%$ of 1\,000 attackers on each small endpoint (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). Against it would run the cross-fitted beta-binomial behind the seasonal gate, which gains on shared stacks everywhere and on smaller new stacks on the small endpoints but loses most new-stack detection on the busiest one (Table~\ref{tab:triggers}). On the busiest endpoint the binomial scope with known fleets as its own trigger would run too: it sits at the budget there, but its false alarms rise by day and it rests on the fleet exemption. The cross-fitted beta-binomial as its own trigger is left out, since it fired in $1.74\%$ of that endpoint's held-out windows. Whether a campaign at the floor exhausts the service depends on its spare capacity, which we did not measure.

\textbf{What each evaluation shows.} The generator shows the mechanism under a mimicry imposed by construction. Its campaigns, a third to half of the window, are easier than the tenth at which production shows the gate failing, and its stacks never occur in legitimate traffic, which is why an unseen filter matches the scope there. Production traffic measures false alarms on real clients, and detection only for an injected botnet of one shape (25 even stacks, new, mostly rare or the endpoint's most common), over nine days of one operator's own endpoints, too few to calibrate a weekly cycle. The five choices of Section~\ref{sec:configs} were made on the days they are reported on, so only the held-out day tests them, and cross-fitting removes only the in-sample fit of the levels. No evaluation covers a captured stealthy campaign, and Appendix~\ref{app:limitations} lists further limits.

\textbf{The scope needs the attack's fingerprint to differ.} A bot outside the threat model that presents a common fingerprint defeats it (Section~\ref{sec:production}). Encrypted Client Hello~\cite{ietf2026ech} leaves the inner \textit{ClientHello} visible to a defender that terminates TLS, as assumed here. The profile must also be kept current: a release that gives many users a new fingerprint would trip the unseen filter at once, which the nine days did not show. We injected neither a flash crowd on one client type, the harder case, nor an attacker who shapes the profile or the fleet exemption. Profiles should therefore be rebuilt from windows the scope did not name, and a new fingerprint that persists should enter them only through an operator.

\textbf{What exchange loses, and what the ontology adds.} Of the standards we examined, STIX~2.1, DOTS and Flowspec have no JA4 property in their core vocabulary, and OCSF records JA4 fingerprints in network events and in the evidence of findings~\cite{ocsf2025schema} but defines no filter or remediation over them. The scope's STIX~2.1 bundles pass the OASIS validator and cross a TAXII~2.1 server, all but their extension definition (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). MISP's importer drops the fingerprints, which STIX~2.1 carries only in an extension that the importer does not map to MISP's own JA4 object. A 2025 extension of the ACL model that DOTS filters copy matches a TLS client's parameters but names no fingerprint, and DOTS does not adopt it~\cite{ietf2025mudtls}. Blocking by fingerprint therefore needs a JA4 match in the DOTS filters and a JA4 property in STIX, for instance in a TLS extension of its network-traffic object. The ontology adds nothing to the detection numbers or to the trigger. It is the specification an operator would deploy, carried into the evidence chain and compiled into the query a log store runs, which reproduced the exported origin and /24-pair counts, and the JA4 pair counts where the WAF blocked no client (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We treated the scope of an application-layer DDoS mitigation as a calibrated statistical decision over the TLS fingerprints of an endpoint's clients, specified in a session ontology. On generated stealthy campaigns, profile-relative scopes block $90\%$ of botnets of up to 25 new stacks with no observed collateral and no label, while the alarm's most common fingerprint blocks users once the botnet spans several stacks. On a CDN operator's traffic, legitimate fleets raise a calibration floor: a 25-stack botnet on rare shared fingerprints is named only past $8\%$ of the busiest endpoint's window and 4 to 19 windows on the small ones. The binomial configuration's false alarms stay at $0.1\%$ of clean windows on the days it was chosen on. A held-out day on which its gate barely opened is consistent with that rate, though the scope misfired in 2 of the 3 windows where the gate did open. On the busiest endpoint the gate lets most of a small botnet through. Post hoc, the unseen filter as its own trigger stops $90\%$ of it on new stacks of $k_{\min}$ origins or more within the budget, and a cross-fitted beta-binomial behind a seasonal gate stops $15\%$ of it on shared stacks but gives up most new-stack detection. Next come the pre-specified test of Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, HTTP/2 attacks and a captured stealthy campaign.

\section{Sub-Relation Specification}
\label{app:algorithms}

The rule's coordination mass is
\begin{equation}
\Omega(S) \;=\; \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}} w_i \,\big|E_i(S)\big| ,
\label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
where $E_i(S)$ is the set of pairs of distinct origins $\{o(s_a), o(s_b)\}$ whose sessions sub-relation $i$ links, $o(s)$ being the source address of session $s$. Each pair counts once, so one client's many connections cannot pose as coordination. The weight $w_i$ follows the attacker's cost of breaking the signal, from the TLS fingerprint ($1.0$) through endpoint convergence ($0.6$) to network proximity ($0.3$), since carrier-grade NAT makes a shared /24 a poor discriminator, and calibration supports only the top of that order (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}). For an equality sub-relation $|E_i(S)| = \sum_k \binom{n_k}{2}$ over the class sizes $n_k$ in origins, so $\Omega$ is a function of the class sizes the compiled query returns (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}).

\textbf{\texttt{TLSFingerprint}:} exact equality of the full JA4, the relation
exercised here. A near variant, annotated \textit{ja4\_distance = 1}, would absorb version drift
within one client library, but it is not an equivalence relation and would need pair
edges, so it is specified but not exercised. \textbf{\texttt{ReusedIdentity}}, \textbf{\texttt{TemporalPattern}} and \textbf{\texttt{PayloadSignature}}, specified but not exercised, match a shared cookie, token or username, inter-arrival sequences within a DTW distance $\tau_{\mathrm{DTW}}$, and payload summaries above a cosine similarity $\tau_{\mathrm{payload}}$. \textbf{\texttt{EndpointConvergence}:} the same endpoint under
path-pattern normalization (\texttt{/api/users/12345} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{/api/users/\{id\}}), annotated \textit{exact = true} on an exact-path match. \textbf{\texttt{NetworkProximity}:} a shared IPv4 /24
(IPv6 /48), a shared ASN from current BGP tables, or both (\textit{strong = true}).

\section{Weight Calibration}
\label{app:calibration}

On the scenarios of our earlier runs, a near-uniform benign vocabulary against a monolithic botnet, maximizing the per-session ROC AUC of
$w_{\mathrm{tls}}\,z(\mathrm{ja4}) + w_{\mathrm{ep}}\,z(\mathrm{endpoint\ conv.})
+ w_{\mathrm{net}}\,z(\mathrm{net\ prox.})$ over the grid $\{0.3\ldots1.0\}^3$
(125 combinations, 60 scenarios) gives $(1.0,\ 0.3,\ 0.3)$. Only the TLS fingerprint discriminates on its own (AUC $0.93$, against $0.50$ for endpoint convergence and $0.58$ for network proximity), and every endpoint weight of the grid attains the same optimal AUC of $0.943$, as do the ontology's weights $(1.0,\ 0.6,\ 0.3)$. Only the top of the order is thus identified, and the per-window rule detects as well with uniform weights (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). Request counts come from all $322\,658$ benign CICIDS2017 sessions and durations from the $40\,351$ with two or more requests. The default scenario's pool of $2\,000$ benign fingerprints takes the Zipf shape of the $495$ measured at one point of presence and exceeds the $76$--$818$ each production endpoint shows. The popularity
measurement is weighted per request, which if anything overstates concentration.

\section{Additional Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\textbf{Instrumentation and deployment.} Counting origins by source address undercounts users behind a carrier-grade NAT and overcounts clients that rotate IPv6 privacy addresses, whose natural unit is the /64. The production run is offline, over aggregated counts per endpoint, so a live CDN deployment still needs the partitioning of Appendix~\ref{app:cost}, which we did not implement.

\textbf{Coverage.} HTTP/2 attacks and other application protocols (DNS, gRPC) need
sub-relations beyond the Slow HTTP DoS family over HTTP/1.1, and $\Omega(S)$
instantiates only the three sub-relations the generator and captures expose, so the weights of the others are untested. We found no independent capture of the stealthy regime: BCCC-cPacket-Cloud-DDoS-2024 criticizes the application-layer coverage of earlier datasets and ships 17 TCP-based DDoS scenarios~\cite{shafi2024bccc}.


\textbf{Laboratory captures and KLAGE.} CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} (Slowloris, Slowhttptest, HULK, GoldenEye) and CIC-IoT2023~\cite{neto2023ciciot} (DDoS Slowloris, HTTP Flood), on which KLAGE reports $F_1 = 84.1\%$ for \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, run through the same extraction pipeline (PCAP $\rightarrow$ JA4 + flows $\rightarrow$ sessions). On CIC-IoT2023 \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, whose attack clusters hold seven origins in one of 143 /24 prefixes, a strong per-session baseline (eight flow attributes) reaches $0.900$ (AUC $0.987$) and the cross-session representation $0.911$ (AUC $0.982$). Real Slowloris, unlike our stealthy campaigns, leaves a per-session flow signature. The comparison with KLAGE is \textit{uncontrolled}: our strong baseline also beats the published $0.841$, and the released code, which starts from a graph of unpublished construction and ships no weights, admits no controlled rerun.

\textbf{Per-session detection and learned models.} Each configuration of this ablation runs on 30 seeds, each campaign split 70/30 into training and test sessions. Configuration (a) uses per-session flow attributes alone, on a deliberately strong Random Forest (RF) repeated with gradient boosting, a perceptron and logistic regression. Configuration (b) adds per-session ontological attributes, (c) uses network proximity only, and (d) the cross-session evidence of the three sub-relations, the share of the cluster sharing one JA4 or /24 plus its size. We also reimplement PCA profiling~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous}, PCA with $k$-means over a behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca} and supervised RF/SVM over session features~\cite{kemp2023approach}. At $K = 50$ and $K = 1000$, (a) reaches a per-session ROC AUC of $0.498$ and $0.503$, (b) $0.500$ and $0.502$, the three academic baselines $0.495$--$0.518$, and (a) stays at $0.488$--$0.503$ under every classifier family ($\alpha = 1.5$, $M = 25$, 30 seeds), the chance level the stealth mode imposes. The cross-session representation (d) reaches $0.927$ and $0.982$, and (c), restricted to network proximity, $0.499$ and $0.659$ (paired Wilcoxon tests, Bonferroni-corrected, $p_{\mathrm{Bonf}} = 7.5\times 10^{-9}$ for (d)$-$(c) and (d)$-$(a) at $K = 1000$).

\textbf{Learned representation.} That separability does not transfer. Fragmenting the botnet over $M$ stacks makes the cross-session evidence non-monotonic in the label: at $K = 1000$ an attacker shares a fingerprint with a few dozen peers, a legitimate client in the benign head with hundreds. Trained on five stacks and tested on 25 or 100, (d) falls to $0.61$ and $0.63$, and trained on 25 to $0.48$ on five and $0.75$ on 100, against $0.95$--$0.995$ on the same $M$ with other seeds. Cross-$M$ tests use disjoint seeds, since the generator draws every benign session before anything $M$-dependent: a same-seed split would score (a) at $0.88$--$0.94$ by memorization.

\section{The Rule per Window and Production Details}
\label{app:window}

Here the rule runs as deployed, per window of $W = 300$~s and endpoint, and no
threshold sees an attack or a label. $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is the 99th
percentile of $\Omega$ over the windows of 30 attack-free runs, which also form the profile. False alarms are counted on 30 more, detection on the default
campaigns ($\alpha = 1.5$, $M = 25$) over windows with at least $k_{\min} = 5$
attackers, and a flash crowd retimes 25, 50 or 100 legitimate sessions of a third
run into one window. We spread benign starts over 60~min, since the generator
packs them into the first minutes.

Over 30 runs each, $\Omega$ passes $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ in $77.8\%$ and $85.7\%$ of the 90 and 35 attack windows at $K = 1000$ and $50$, in $0.6\%$ of 360 clean windows and in $80$--$100\%$ of the flash crowds of 25 to 100 users. A flash crowd adds mass as a campaign does. The test tells them apart because these crowds draw users from the benign mix the profile
describes, while a surge on one client type is enriched, as production fleets show. With the test the rule fires on no clean window
(one-sided $95\%$ upper bound $0.8\%$) and no flash crowd, and blocks a median $75$--$80\%$ of
the attackers in every attack window $\Omega$ flags, hitting no legitimate session. With uniform weights $\Omega$ catches at least as many attack windows
($78.9\%$ and $85.7\%$), and without its endpoint term $33.3\%$ and none, while still flagging $53$--$100\%$ of flash crowds. A distinct-origin gate catches $82.2\%$ and $85.7\%$, and with the test no clean window or
flash crowd.

\textbf{Production traffic.} The exports carry no prefixes, so the scope is judged on JA4 alone. At the nominal level the scope names a filter in $60.1\%$ of clean windows counted in connections, since one client opens many, and in $26.9\%$ counted in origins, because legitimate fleets switch on together. Calibrating $\lambda_e$ on the preceding days brings the base rule to $0.2\%$, and the distinct-origin gate, which fires with $\Omega$ in 136 of the 181 clean windows where either fires, halves its false alarms with no loss of detection. At $\lambda_e = 10^{-60}$ the binomial test names a stack only past 15 to 18 origins. Fleets, such as a probe fleet active only in business hours, push $\lambda_e$ down, and without fleets exempted the test alone names a filter in $1.5$--$6.3\%$ of the next day's windows, often a fleet blocking about half of a window's clients.

\textbf{Details of V-B.} In attack windows the calibrated scopes block a median $0\%$ of legitimate clients and up to $71\%$ in the worst window of a small endpoint. Gate and scope misfire together more often than independence predicts for the post hoc beta-binomial and the calibrated $z$-score, in 22 and 20 windows against 7.5 and 11.9 ($P < 10^{-4}$ and $P = 0.02$ for a Poisson count), and in 6 and 13 against 1.8 and 4.6 cross-fitted. For the binomial configuration the excess is within chance, 5 against 3.5 and 6 against 3.2 cross-fitted ($P = 0.27$ and $0.10$). The expectation pools each endpoint's days, so day-to-day swings alone can inflate the excess. The misfires of the other two also cluster by day, 13 of each on one day of the console, so exact intervals, which assume independent windows, understate their uncertainty. Under the binomial in sample, the shared-stack floor for 5 and 100 stacks is 50 and 1\,026 attackers on E1, 164 and 6\,158 on E2, 28 and 1\,017 on E3 and 17 and 454 on E4. For 25 stacks, fingerprints of rank 11 to 35 need 808 on E3 and 114 on E4, and those of rank 36 to 100 need 743, 1\,898, 227 and 84.

\textbf{Details of V-C.} Pooled, the binomial configuration stops $38.4\%$ and $77.7\%$ of 100 and 1\,000 attackers on new stacks and $21.7\%$ and $63.3\%$ on shared ones, figures the small endpoints dominate, and $38.9\%$, $73.4\%$, $21.5\%$ and $60.9\%$ on the held-out day. On the busiest endpoint, depending on the day, the gate opens in $0.7$--$42\%$ of the windows of a botnet a tenth the size of the window. It opens in $45\%$ at a third and in $79\%$ at a whole window. Of 100 attackers on new stacks, the unseen filter by itself behind the origin gate stops $29.4\%$, the calibrated $z$-score $54.7\%$ ($23.7\%$ on shared ones) and the post hoc beta-binomial $59.8\%$ ($40.5\%$). Fingerprints the profile has never seen appear every day but rarely concentrate: $0.21\%$ of the test days' windows hold one with at least $k_{\min}$ origins. As its own trigger the unseen filter's misfires cluster by day: all 4 on the busiest endpoint and both on the console fall on one day, and 5 of the API's 6 on another. On each small endpoint every scope of Table~\ref{tab:triggers} stops $90.0\%$ of 1\,000 attackers on new stacks under every trigger, on the test days and the held-out day.

\textbf{Details of V-D.} On the held-out day the base rule, the protocol's reference, fired on the same two windows as the configuration, as did the calibrated $z$-score, outside the protocol. That day Table~\ref{tab:triggers}'s shared-stack cells read 0.0 (origin gate), 11.9, 14.3 and 0.0 (seasonal gate) and 66.1, 83.3 and 0.0 (no gate), and its flash-crowd cells $24.5$--$25.1\%$ for the calibrated scopes against $0.1\%$ for the unseen filter. The configuration's 280 firings on the single sign-on's 1\,000-user crowds that day name one fingerprint in $98.2\%$ of cases, and its filters there block a median $1.1\%$ of the window's clients. The same crowds trigger the post hoc seasonal gate's cross-fitted beta-binomial in $97.6\%$ of that day's windows. Post hoc, on ranks 36 to 100 the busiest endpoint's floor falls from 743 attackers under the binomial to 480 under the beta-binomial in sample, and rises to 949 cross-fitted. Against the binomial in sample, the cross-fitted beta-binomial's floor on those ranks is also higher on the API (255 against 227), equal on the single sign-on and lower on the console (509 against 1\,898). Cross-fitted, post hoc, the calibrated $z$-score stops $33.6\%$ of 100 attackers against $54.7\%$ in sample. The beta-binomial's levels then fall to between $10^{-17}$ and $10^{-2}$, its scope alone fires in $1.2\%$ of the held-out day's windows, and one known fleet appears on the busiest endpoint on three of the five days. Most clients the WAF blocked sat on fingerprints that unblocked clients also present. With the profile built without them, $84$--$99\%$ of the clients the scopes would block on the full traffic of the API and single sign-on were blocked by the WAF. With the profile of all clients, the scopes catch the WAF's surges clearly above chance only on the console, and there with under $1\%$ of the blocked clients.

\textbf{Other baselines.} Uncalibrated and behind the $\Omega$ gate, the $z$-score fires on $2.6\%$ of clean
windows and $64.5\%$ of flash crowds of 100 users ($86.8\%$ of 1\,000). Flash crowds of 1\,000 users trigger the base rule in $21.8\%$ of windows. When they trigger the binomial configuration, its filters block a median $7.1\%$, $18.6\%$ and $2.7\%$ of the window's clients on E2 to E4, and $20.6\%$ at the 90th percentile.

\textbf{Known fleets and the scope as a trigger.} Of the four known-fleet shares tried on the first three test days, $5\%$ was the
only one whose false alarms did not exceed the base rule's (5 against 6, the others 12 to 16). By the rate times the median collateral, a rougher measure than the mean, the $0.5\%$ share blocked about 25 times fewer legitimate clients per clean window on those days ($0.0028\%$ against $0.070\%$). It also stopped more of 100 attackers on new stacks ($44.6\%$ against $30.9\%$). Post hoc, a trigger on the scope
alone fires, with known fleets, on $2.2\%$ of pooled clean windows and
$0.5\%$ on the held-out day, against $3.5\%$ and $8.8\%$ for the union without them. With known fleets it stops $61.1\%$ of the busiest endpoint's tenth-size botnet on shared stacks, and its misfires there block a median $1.0\%$ of the clients. Against the post hoc beta-binomial, under which no fingerprint qualifies as a known fleet in sample, the busiest endpoint's level rises to about $10^{-4}$ and the others' to the $0.01$ cap. There the scope alone stops $90.0\%$ and $78.3\%$ of a botnet a tenth the size of the busiest window, on new and shared stacks, at $2.1\%$ of that endpoint's clean windows. The post hoc seasonal gate alone fires on $3.0\%$ of clean test-day windows, as the origin gate does, and on the busiest endpoint stops $10.0$--$44.7\%$ of a tenth-size botnet depending on the day. Setting $\rho = 2$ or $5$ moves no pooled firing rate of the base rule by over $0.3$ points, no blocked share by over $4.5$ and no collateral median by over $7.2$.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The reference implementation is available at
\url{https://github.com/Natanael0liveira/knowledge-graph-ddos-article} under the
MIT license. \texttt{make compile-check stix-ingest} checks the compiled
count query on generated sessions and passes the exported bundles through the
OASIS STIX~2.1 validator in strict mode, the reference TAXII~2.1 server (medallion 3.0.0), which returns them unchanged save the extension definition it fails to serve, and MISP's importer (misp-stix 2026.9.16). The compiled query returned the exported origin and /24-pair counts in every window of two production days, 1\,152 windows each, and the JA4 pair counts in the 288 windows a day of the endpoint where the WAF blocked no client. Elsewhere the export drops the clients the WAF partly blocked, which the compiled filter keeps. The protocol named the JA4 class sizes, and the check compared their pair counts, which constrain the per-fingerprint counts the scope tests but do not identify them. It ran in the operator's log store, the artifact keeps only its counts, and the production evaluation reads the exports. The held-out
day's protocol was written down, with the hashes of its exports, before the day
was read (\texttt{fresh\_day\_protocol.md}). It was committed 17 hours after the
time it states, so no external timestamp attests that order. Seeds are fixed, so each table and figure of generated traffic regenerates from one command. The production tables (\texttt{make production-tables waf-labels}) regenerate from per-window results kept with the operator's data, and \texttt{make audit} recomputes about 250 of the printed numbers from the anonymized summaries in the repository. We intend to submit the artifacts to the Artifact Evaluation track.
