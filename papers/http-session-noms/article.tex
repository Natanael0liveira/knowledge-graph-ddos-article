\begin{abstract}
Distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaigns keep every source below any per-session
threshold, so the attack signal lives in the structure \textit{between} sessions.
Detectors discard that structure by reducing sessions to vectors, and recent
knowledge-graph detectors reason about network nodes and stop at a text report. We model the HTTP session as a first-class entity in OWL, linked to other
sessions by six typed sub-properties weighted by the attacker's cost of breaking
each signal. A SPARQL/SWRL rule fires on their weighted mass, and its derivation
is the verdict, exported as a JSON-LD and STIX~2.1 evidence chain whose
discriminator scopes the mitigation. Against stealthy campaigns from botnets of
compromised devices, per-session detection stays at chance across four
classifier families, while the cross-session representation reaches ROC AUC
$0.98$. On public datasets, whose attacks carry obvious flow signatures, a
strong per-session baseline suffices. Scoping mitigation by the property
most of the cluster shares selects a \textit{legitimate} fingerprint once the
botnet spans several TLS stacks, blocking $0\%$ of the attack and $39\%$ of
legitimate traffic. Enrichment-based scoping blocks $85$--$90\%$
with no collateral observed. As a detector, the symbolic path needs no training,
yet recovers two to five times more of a fragmented campaign than a learned
model at zero false positives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Application-layer Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks continue to grow in volume and frequency. In August 2023, HTTP/2 Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487) pushed mitigation peaks to 398 million requests per second from a botnet of roughly twenty thousand machines~\cite{cve202344487,google2023rapidreset}, and Cloudflare blocked more than 6500 hyper-volumetric attacks in Q2 2025 alone, with HTTP DDoS growing 129\% year over year~\cite{cloudflare2025q2ddos}. Attacks of this magnitude are, however, what volumetric defenses are built to absorb, and detection becomes harder at the low-rate end of the spectrum. There sits the family of \textit{distributed Slow HTTP DoS} attacks: Slowloris and its descendants, plus rate-based variants that still hold connections open, such as HULK and GoldenEye~\cite{cambiaso2013slowdos}. They exhaust a server from several coordinated origins, each sending requests small enough that no per-source threshold is crossed, which is what makes them difficult to identify. %Modern HTTP/2 attacks share that multi-origin exhaustion property, but they operate through distinct mechanisms, notably stream cancellation and header continuation abuse. Those call for sub-relations outside the scope we evaluate here, and we treat them as the immediate extension target (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) rather than as a claim of this paper.

A stealthy campaign also shapes each session to look legitimate on the per-session signals detectors rely on, such as connection duration and route entropy~\cite{kemp2023approach}. What remains discriminative lives in the structure \textit{between} sessions: the same client fingerprint recurring across many origins, and those origins converging on one endpoint. Framed this way, the problem belongs to network and service management as much as to detection, because acting on it requires a decision: \textit{which} clients to act on, on \textit{what} evidence, and with a filter narrow enough to spare the legitimate users of the attacked service.

Existing work addresses the detection side of this problem and leaves the operational side open. A meta-analysis of 75 studies finds that 47\% of application-layer DDoS detectors derive their features from HTTP sessions~\cite{odusami2020survey}, and those sessions are flattened into a numeric vector before reaching the classifier. The detector then sees statistics about sessions, and cannot reason about fingerprints recurring across origins or about patterns that exist only between individually normal sessions. Graph-based detectors keep such relations: KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage} builds knowledge graphs from network logs, classifies nodes with Graph-BERT~\cite{zhang2020graphbertattentionneededlearning} and reports in natural language, reaching $F_1 = 84.1\%$ on IoT benchmarks with DDoS Slowloris. Three opportunities remain open. First, the reasoning unit is the \textit{network node} (port, IP, flow), while Layer-7 coordination becomes visible only at the level of the application session. Second, coordination is captured implicitly, in learned embeddings, so no operator can inspect the relations behind the verdict. Third, the output stops at a report, whereas acting on it demands a mitigation policy whose scope follows from the graph. These three gaps define the space this paper occupies.

This paper addresses them with a knowledge-graph solution to detect and mitigate application-layer DDoS. It captures live HTTP/TLS traffic, extracts fingerprint, flow and endpoint information from each request, and resolves the request to an \texttt{ApplicationSession} node of an OWL graph kept over a sliding window. Sessions in that window are linked by six typed sub-properties weighted by the attacker's cost of breaking each signal, and a SPARQL/SWRL rule fires on the weighted mass of those links. The operator therefore receives the derivation that satisfied the rule, and the discriminator it names becomes the scope of the mitigation. We target campaigns run from botnets of compromised devices, whose TLS stacks their operator cannot cheaply replace, and evaluate bots that present a browser's fingerprint as the boundary of the method. This paper makes four contributions. \1~We propose an OWL ontology in which the HTTP session is a first-class entity, related to other sessions by \textit{six typed sub-properties} weighted by evasion cost, so that network proximity, which modern botnets break trivially, enters only as auxiliary evidence. \2~We define a decision procedure in which a SPARQL/SWRL rule fires on the weighted coordination mass $\Omega(S)$ of a candidate cluster, so that the reported verdict is the derivation that satisfied the rule. \3~We derive the mitigation scope from the same graph and export it with its evidence chain in JSON-LD and STIX~2.1. We show that the natural choice, the property most of the cluster shares, is harmful under realistic conditions, and replace it with an enrichment criterion over a profile of normal traffic. \4~We evaluate the approach against a strong per-session baseline as the campaign grows more distributed, and measure the false-positive rate of the resulting mitigation filter as collateral damage on legitimate traffic.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}


Table~\ref{tab:related} compares this work with prior art on four dimensions: the reasoning unit, the representation of relations between sessions, the form of the explanation, and whether a mitigation scope and its collateral cost follow from that representation. The subsections cover security knowledge graphs, Layer-7 detection, and how both treat sessions.


\subsection{Knowledge Graphs for Security and Management}

The first wave of security knowledge graphs was \textit{static}: entities extracted from Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE), reports and blogs, normalized and linked into a queryable base~\cite{jia2018practical,bonagiri2024practical}. A survey of the area states the resulting gap~\cite{liu2022recent}: ``\textit{it is still unclear how to implement the knowledge graph to face real industrial difficulties in cyber attack and defense situations}''. Graphs built from threat intelligence text do not capture runtime traffic structure.

More recent work populates graphs from the traffic itself, and KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage} is the closest of these to the approach proposed here. Graph representations are also gaining ground in adjacent management tasks: one line identifies device dependencies through link prediction over network graphs~\cite{sadlek2024device}, another learns attack action sequences from testbed measurements~\cite{wang2024intrusion}. Both operate on network-level entities, and neither derives a mitigation scope from what it finds.

\subsection{Layer-7 HTTP DDoS Detection}
%, over a body of surveys that consolidate the domain~
% builds baselines from aggregated flow and session statistics

The state of the art on Layer-7 HTTP DDoS detection~\cite{tripathi2021application,cambiaso2013slowdos} falls into two categories: \1~statistical profiling and \2~Machine Learning (ML). The first applies Principal Component Analysis (PCA) either to flow statistics~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous} or to a per-session behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca}, the latter being among the first formulations to put the session at the center, though still as a feature vector. The second and more recent category applies supervised learning over session features~\cite{kemp2023approach}, whose authors note that the method was not validated in real time and that its output is binary, with no ontological explanation. Neither category retains a relation between sessions: whatever coordination exists is dissolved into the feature vector before the classifier ever sees it.


\subsection{Session Modeling in Anomaly Detection}

Among the 75 studies of a meta-analysis on the topic~\cite{odusami2020survey}, none keeps the session as an object with identity, target, behavior and typed relations. The reduction is uniform, and it puts cross-session reasoning beyond the detector's reach.

Industrial bot-management platforms do exploit cross-session structure: signals aggregated per JA4 fingerprint over all recent traffic feed bot scoring and DDoS protection~\cite{martinetti2024ja4signals}. They are proprietary, however, with no public ontology, no reproducible academic benchmark, and no formalized collateral-damage metric.

Table~\ref{tab:related} makes the boundary explicit. Statistical and machine-learning detectors reduce the session to a vector, so no relation between sessions survives, and no mitigation scope or cost can follow from the decision. Graph-based detectors keep relations, but on network-level entities and in learned form, which yields a classification and at best an explanation reconstructed after the fact. Industrial platforms act on per-fingerprint aggregates and disclose neither the representation nor the cost of acting on it. No published approach, therefore, holds three properties together: the application session as the reasoning unit, explicit and typed relations between sessions, and a mitigation scope derived from the representation that produced the verdict, with its collateral damage reported. The rest of this paper supplies that combination and measures what it costs when the scope is wrong.

\subsection{Employing Knowledge Graphs}

A knowledge graph states facts as RDF triples $\langle$subject, predicate,
object$\rangle$, and an OWL~2 ontology declares the classes and properties they
may use. We work in the OWL~2~RL profile~\cite{w3c2012owl2profiles}, whose
polynomial forward-chaining entailment makes bounded materialization tractable
for runtime reasoning where OWL~2~DL is not. Materialization runs once per
operational window, off the per-request path. SWRL adds Horn rules, which assert a
triple when a conjunction of premises holds. SPARQL queries the result and
aggregates over solution sets, which SWRL cannot do. Client identity comes from
JA4~\cite{althouse2023ja4}, a hash of the TLS \textit{ClientHello} that identifies
a client's TLS stack. Being independent of the source address, it survives IP
rotation.

\subsection{Defining the Ontology}
\label{sec:ontology}

The central class is \texttt{ApplicationSession}, related by
\texttt{hasIdentity} to a composite \texttt{Identity} (cookie, JWT token,
username, TLS fingerprint), by \texttt{targets} to an \texttt{Endpoint}
(\texttt{AuthEndpoint}, \texttt{APIEndpoint}, \texttt{StaticAsset}), by
\texttt{exhibitsBehavior} to a behavioral profile, and by \texttt{mitigatedBy}
to a policy (\texttt{RateLimit}, \texttt{Challenge}, \texttt{Block}) carrying a
\texttt{scope} attribute. The abstract class \texttt{ApplicationLayerAttack}
specializes into \texttt{SlowHTTPDoSFamily}, itself split into
\texttt{ConnectionExhaustionAttack} (Slowloris, slow body, slow read) and
\texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood} (HULK, GoldenEye). The ontology is aligned with
STIX~2.1~\cite{oasis2021stix} and MITRE ATT\&CK, and also declares
\texttt{CredentialStuffing} and \texttt{CoordinatedAPIAbuse}, which reuse the
same machinery but are outside the experimental scope of this paper.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

The defender operates the attacked application and observes, per request, the TLS
\textit{ClientHello} (hence a JA4 fingerprint), source address and prefix/ASN,
target endpoint, arrival time, and whatever identity material the application
exposes. It sees neither the botnet's control channel nor which sources are
coordinated, but can profile the service's own traffic outside attack episodes.
The attacker controls $K$ compromised devices and may shape each session to be
statistically indistinguishable from a legitimate one, the stealth assumption of
our evaluation. Timing, identities and payloads can be jittered, rotated or
randomized, each at an operational cost that the weighting of
Section~\ref{sec:family} encodes. The TLS stack is harder to change, because it
is fixed by the library each device ships, as in the router, camera and DVR
botnets of the Mirai lineage~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}. Presenting a browser's
\textit{ClientHello} would require shipping an impersonation library to every
device and keeping it in step with browser releases. Bots that do so, such as
headless-browser farms, fall outside this model, and Sections~\ref{sec:collateral}
and~\ref{sec:discussion} evaluate them as its boundary.

\subsection{The Weighted \texttt{relatedTo} Family}
\label{sec:family}


Cross-session relatedness is a structured family of relations, declared
via \texttt{rdfs:subPropertyOf} under a transitive \texttt{relatedTo} and
annotated with a \texttt{coordinationWeight} $w_i \in [0,1]$, depicted in
Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology}. Each is instantiated independently from its own observed
evidence, so two sessions may be linked by one, several or all six.

The ordering is an evasion-cost argument for that attacker. Changing the TLS
stack means replacing the library on every device, and JA4 sorts extensions
before hashing, so it stays stable under the extension randomization Chrome
introduced in 2023, which breaks classic JA3~\cite{althouse2023ja4}. Identity reuse is equally expensive to break, because
spreading a campaign over many credentials undermines its economics. Temporal
coherence originates in how the botnet is operated, and random jitter can
partially break it. Payload and endpoint
signals are intermediate: randomizing them is cheap, but doing so costs campaign
coherence. Network proximity is the weakest link by construction: Mirai
derivatives, cloud fleets and residential-proxy networks spread across ASNs and
prefixes, and carrier-grade NAT makes a shared /24 a poor discriminator. The calibration in
Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} corroborates the ordering, and makes no
claim about the absolute values.

\subsection{Runtime Graph Construction}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Sessions and relations live inside a sliding operational window ($W = 5$~min by
default) and are purged incrementally, following an RDF stream-processing
discipline, with materialization over Apache Jena Fuseki and TDB2 storage.
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} lays out the two layers and the rates they run at, and
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms} gives the construction algorithm and the per-pair
procedure of each \texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation. Exact JA4, endpoint and prefix
matches are equivalence relations, so each partitions $S$ into classes and
$|E_i(S)| = \sum_k \binom{n_k}{2}$ over their sizes. Admitting a session then
increments one class counter per such sub-relation, at a cost independent of
$|S_W|$, and $\Omega(S)$ needs no pair edge. Only the non-transitive relations
(near-variant JA4, temporal, payload) compare candidate pairs, which prefix
indexes and locality-sensitive hashing restrict. Section~\ref{sec:latency}
measures both paths against window occupancy.

\subsection{Detection Rule}
\label{sec:rule}

Let $V_{\mathrm{sess}}$ be the sessions active in $W$. For a subset $S \subseteq
V_{\mathrm{sess}}$ and a candidate endpoint $e$, define the \textit{coordination
mass}

\begin{equation}
\Omega(S) \;=\; \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}} w_i \,\big|E_i(S)\big| ,
\label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
with $E_i(S) = \{\{s_a,s_b\} \subseteq S : (s_a,\texttt{relatedBy}_i,s_b) \in E\}$
the set of session pairs linked by sub-relation $i$. Sub-relations are symmetric,
so each pair counts once. The rule
\texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood} holds when there exists $S$ with (i)
$|S| \geq k_{\min}$; (ii) $\forall s \in S: (s,\texttt{targets},e) \in E$; (iii)
$\Omega(S) \geq \tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$; (iv) aggregate rate on $e$ above
$\tau_{\mathrm{rate}}$; and (v) a coherent \texttt{BotBehavior} profile.

Condition (iii) is the contribution. $\Omega(S)$ can clear the threshold with
\texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity} at zero, the distributed-botnet case,
provided some combination of high-weight signals is present. Conversely, a set
held together \textit{only} by network proximity, such as mobile users
behind one CGN /24, needs three times as many linked pairs to reach the same
$\Omega$ as a TLS-linked set. Since $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is calibrated on
benign clusters, ordinary CGN aggregation stays below it, which protects against
a well-known false-positive mode.

\subsection{Symbolic Formulation}
\label{sec:symbolic}

Reasoning is symbolic in two deliberate stages. SWRL rules
instantiate the \texttt{relatedBy} family pair by pair over predicates the
evidence extractors produce: the equality-based
relations are pure Horn, while numeric ones such as DTW distance and payload
cosine similarity are evaluated beforehand and enter the rule as a built-in
comparison against their threshold. Aggregation is not Horn, because SWRL
cannot sum, so $\Omega(S)$ is computed by a SPARQL query that reads the weights
from the \texttt{coordinationWeight} annotations of the ontology and, for the
equality-based relations, counts pairs from class sizes.
Listing~\ref{lst:rules} shows one rule of each kind.


\subsection{Evidence Chain and Derived Mitigation Scope}
\label{sec:evidence}

When the rule fires, the engine emits the satisfied rule, the instances
involved, the decomposition of $\Omega(S)$ per sub-relation, and the derived
mitigation scope. We export it as JSON-LD and as
STIX~2.1, an \textit{indicator} plus a \textit{course-of-action} linked by
\textit{mitigates}, for SIEM and SOAR ingestion.

How the scope is chosen decides whether the verdict is useful or harmful, and the
frequency-based choice fails. The modal fingerprint of the cluster selects whatever is
common, and on a service under attack what is common is the legitimate
population. Against a botnet spread over several TLS stacks, each attacker stack
is smaller than the head of the benign distribution, so the modal value is a
\textit{legitimate} fingerprint and the resulting filter blocks users and no
attackers (Section~\ref{sec:collateral}).

We therefore select the discriminator by \textbf{enrichment}. Let $c(f)$ be
the prevalence of fingerprint $f$ inside the fired
cluster and $b(f)$ its prevalence in a background profile of normal traffic
maintained by the operator outside attack episodes. The scope admits every $f$
with $c(f)/b(f) \geq \rho$ and $c(f) \geq \sigma$, yielding a \textit{set} of
fingerprints, which is what covers a fragmented botnet. The
support floor $\sigma$ must sit below the share of the smallest stack worth acting
on, that is, below $1/M$ for a botnet of $M$ stacks. Lowering it costs nothing,
because it is the enrichment test that keeps legitimate traffic out. We use $\rho = 3$ and $\sigma = 0.002$.

The background must come from outside the attack episode, because the campaign
spans the whole window and would otherwise give every candidate an enrichment of
one. No labels are involved: the profile is what a defender sees in normal
operation. An adversary hiding inside a popular benign fingerprint is therefore
not enriched, so the framework refuses the scope and reports that no
discriminator exists.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section details the evaluation methodology. We first describe the scenarios and the traffic sources behind them, both the calibrated synthetic generator and the public captures, together with the parameters that decide how closely a scenario resembles production. We then present the baselines, the ablation configurations that isolate the representation's contribution, and the metrics every result is reported in.

\subsection{Scenarios and Datasets}

The central hypothesis has two parts: cross-session representation gains over
per-session detection as the \textit{degree of distribution} $K$ of the campaign
grows, and the symbolic path turns that structure into an auditable,
low-collateral decision. The evaluation is therefore parameterized by $K$.
\textbf{Scenario~A} ($K=1$) is the classic single-source flood, which volumetric
detectors already handle and which is degenerate for the per-session area under
the ROC curve (AUC). \textbf{Scenario~B} ($10 \leq K \leq 100$) models small
botnets whose origins each stay inside their own rate limit, so network
proximity is partial and high-weight signals must carry detection.
\textbf{Scenario~C} ($K \geq 1000$) spreads the campaign over hundreds of ASNs
and prefixes, leaving negligible per-session signal and a nearly empty
\texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity}. This is where the contribution must
hold. We report B ($K=50$) and C ($K=1000$).

\textbf{Primary evaluation: calibrated synthetic traffic.} A generator produces
distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaigns, the family of
Section~\ref{sec:ontology}: sustained low-rate sessions against one endpoint,
with known coordination ground truth, parameterized by $K$,
by the fraction of origins sharing TLS signature and identity, by IP/ASN
dispersion and by the time window.

Two parameters of that generator decide whether the scenario resembles
production: benign fingerprint popularity, which we measure, and botnet
composition, which no published measurement fixes and we therefore sweep. The
first is shaped as a Zipf curve of exponent $\alpha$: real
populations are concentrated, since an up-to-date browser on a given platform
emits essentially one JA4~\cite{althouse2023ja4}. We
calibrated that curve against $6.33$M TLS requests logged at a production edge
point of presence of Azion Technologies\footnote{Measured 22 August 2026 from an
access log the platform already collects. The extract holds only client-software
fingerprints and their frequencies, with no address, host, URI, header or
locale.}: $495$ distinct fingerprints, the most common accounting for $38.4\%$ of
requests and the top ten for $93.8\%$. The measured curve is bracketed by
$\alpha = 1.5$ and $\alpha = 2.0$. We take $\alpha = 1.5$ as canonical and sweep
the range. As for botnet composition, real botnets span device classes
such as routers, IP cameras and DVRs~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}, and therefore
TLS stacks, so we distribute the campaign uniformly over $M$ stacks, the
conservative choice for a given $M$, and take $M = 25$ as canonical. An \textit{adversarial} mode lets the botnet adopt
the most common benign fingerprints, as an impersonating bot would, and marks the
boundary of the threat model.
Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} reports the measurement and its caveats.

Legitimate distributions are calibrated against the $\sim$322k benign sessions of
CICIDS2017 and verified by Kolmogorov--Smirnov
tests: $D = 0.003$ for duration and $D = 0.002$ for request count, both with
$p > 0.8$, so the gain cannot come from easy benign traffic. A \textit{stealth}
mode draws attacker sessions from the same distributions as benign traffic, so
the campaign reveals itself only through cross-session structure.

\textbf{Real data.} CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} (Wednesday capture:
Slowloris, Slowhttptest, HULK, GoldenEye) and
CIC-IoT2023~\cite{neto2023ciciot}, the latter being the dataset on which KLAGE
reports $F_1 = 84.1\%$ for \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, run through the same
extraction pipeline (PCAP $\rightarrow$ JA4 + flows $\rightarrow$ sessions
$\rightarrow$ graph). Both are laboratory testbed captures, a caveat we
return to in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Baselines, Configurations and Metrics}

Each ablation configuration is run with $n = 30$ distinct seeds, giving bootstrap
intervals and paired tests. All configurations consume the same
traffic attributes, so the ablation separates \textit{representation},
not data availability. $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is fixed per scenario at the
99th percentile of $\Omega$ over legitimate clusters.

The per-session baseline is deliberately \textbf{strong}: a Random Forest (RF) over
all eight to nine available flow attributes (request count and duration, byte
and packet sums in both directions, mean and standard deviation of
inter-request time). To keep the negative result from depending on one
hypothesis class, we repeat it with histogram gradient boosting, a multilayer
perceptron and logistic regression over the same features. We also reimplement
three academic baselines: PCA profiling~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous}, PCA followed by $k$-means clustering over a behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca}, and supervised RF/SVM over session
features~\cite{kemp2023approach}. We compare against published KLAGE
numbers on the same dataset and attack. The four ablation configurations are:
\textbf{(a)} ML baseline without ontology; \textbf{(b)} ontology with \textit{no}
\texttt{relatedBy} sub-relation, sessions as isolated nodes; \textbf{(c)}
ontology with \textit{only} \texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity}, emulating a naive
ASN/prefix detector; and \textbf{(d)} the full cross-session representation over the three
sub-relations observable in the evaluated traffic. Thus (d)$-$(a) is the total contribution, (d)$-$(b) the
gain of representing cross-session structure, and (d)$-$(c) the gain
of high-weight signals over network proximity.

Configuration (d) consumes the cross-session evidence as \textit{features}: the
share of a cluster sharing one JA4 or /24, plus its size. This
feature-level proxy for the \texttt{relatedBy} family keeps every configuration
equal in data and classifier.

Beyond standard classification metrics we report \textbf{collateral damage}: the
fraction of legitimate traffic falling inside the scope derived from the verdict.
It is the false-positive rate of the filter an operator would deploy, which can
differ sharply from that of the detector (Section~\ref{sec:collateral}).
Evidence chains are assessed for \textit{completeness} (do they enumerate every
correlated session and activated sub-relation?) and \textit{actionability} (is the
derived scope coherent with the cluster discriminator?).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The results follow the claims they support. We begin with the ablation that isolates the representation under stealthy distributed campaigns, then measure the symbolic rule directly as a detector, with no classifier trained on its features, and confront both with real traffic and with the closest published system. The remaining subsections quantify what the verdict costs once it is acted on: the collateral damage of the derived scope, the runtime cost of each layer and the choice of $W$, and the statistical significance of the comparisons.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the per-session AUC over stealthy distributed
campaigns in which legitimate users access the \textit{same} endpoint on the same
port as the attack, so neither flow volume nor destination port separates the
two classes. The pattern survives a strong baseline: per-session detection (a) with the full flow-feature set, the three
academic baselines, and configuration (b), which adds per-session ontological
attributes but no cross-session relation, all sit at chance
($\mathrm{AUC}\approx 0.50$), because each attacker session is by construction
drawn from the benign distributions. The cross-session representation (d) reaches
$0.93$--$0.98$. Configuration (c), restricted to network proximity, is weak
($0.50$--$0.66$) because the botnet is dispersed.

Across the four hypothesis classes, configuration (a) stays at chance for every
family, at $0.471$--$0.503$, which locates the collapse in the representation:
no hypothesis class recovers what the features discarded.

Configuration (d) is where model choice starts to matter, and the realistic
scenario exposes why. Fragmenting the botnet over 25 stacks makes the
cross-session evidence \textit{non-monotonic} in the label: an attacker shares a
fingerprint with a few dozen peers, while a legitimate client in the head of the
benign distribution shares it with hundreds. Tree ensembles carve out the
intermediate band and reach $0.98$--$0.99$ at $K = 1000$ and $0.92$--$0.93$ at
$K = 50$. Logistic regression, restricted to a monotone response, drops to
$0.80$ and $0.87$, and the perceptron inverts outright at $K = 50$
($\mathrm{AUC} = 0.235$), having learned that a widely shared fingerprint
indicates benign traffic.

The non-monotonicity is an argument for the symbolic path. A learned model must
infer the benign baseline from its training sample and encode it in a boundary,
while the rule of Section~\ref{sec:evidence} compares against an explicit
profile. Enrichment therefore behaves uniformly
across the fragmentation range where the learners diverge.

That (b) sits at chance too shows per-session attributes are not enough: the
\textit{explicit relation between} sessions is what carries detection. The mass
of $\Omega(S)$ that fires the rule is dominated by
\texttt{relatedByTLSFingerprint} ($w=1.0$) and endpoint convergence, while
\texttt{relatedByNetworkProximity} does not activate in dispersed campaigns. We
also assessed evidence-chain quality over seven real clusters: all list
the satisfied rule, the activated sub-relations and weights, the
$\Omega(S)$ score and derived scope, and yield a usable
filter (Listing~\ref{lst:chain}, Appendix~\ref{app:cost}). A human-analyst study is future work.

Every number so far came from a classifier over cross-session features, which
says nothing about the symbolic layer. We therefore evaluate the rule directly:
the sessions matching the scope derived in Section~\ref{sec:evidence} \textit{are}
the flagged set, with no model training and no learned score threshold, and the
decision arrives with its derivation. Since AUC is a ranking measure and the rule
emits a hard decision, we also force the classifier onto the rule's own operating
point (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}).

Against a monolithic or lightly fragmented botnet the learned model is competitive at zero
false positives ($91.7\%$ and $88.6\%$ recall against $84.0\%$ and $90.0\%$):
there is no symbolic advantage in the easy regime. From 25 stacks on, the rule
pulls clearly ahead: $90.3\%$ against $36.4\%$, and $85.0\%$ against $17.6\%$ at one
hundred stacks, and the rule holds that lead even against a model allowed to
spend false positives: at $1\%$ FPR the model reaches only $66.8\%$ and $44.5\%$.
The adversarial row is \textit{not} a like-for-like comparison and we make no claim
from it: there the rule itself incurs a $3.78\%$ false-positive rate, and the model reaches $23.4\%$
once allowed $1\%$, so the ordering at a matched operating point is unresolved.

AUC alone does not capture this operating-point behavior. The classifier still scores $0.979$
and $0.961$ where it recovers a third and a sixth of the attack: its ranking is
good, but its scores overlap benign traffic in the tail, so no threshold yields
automatic action without collateral. The rule produces no score to threshold,
only a set defined by an enrichment test against an explicit background, so
its zero false-positive rate in these scenarios follows from that criterion
with no tuned threshold behind it. That is not an unconditional guarantee.

\subsection{Collateral Damage on Legitimate Traffic}
\label{sec:collateral}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig3_collateral.png}
\caption{Scope derivation: frequency versus enrichment ($\alpha = 1.5$, $n = 15$).}
\label{fig:collateral}
\end{figure}

Mitigation is only meaningful as a pair: what the derived scope blocks, and what
it costs. We report both over $n = 15$ campaigns per configuration, comparing the
frequency rule against the enrichment rule of Section~\ref{sec:evidence}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:collateral}).

For a monolithic botnet the two agree: $84.0\%$ of attacker sessions blocked with
no collateral observed, against $100\%$ for a global endpoint rate limit, which by
definition disconnects every legitimate user of the attacked service. That is the
regime in which the frequency rule was validated, and it is not representative.
From five stacks on, the modal fingerprint of the cluster becomes a
\textit{legitimate} one and the rule inverts: $0.0\%$ of the attack blocked and
$39.0\%$ of legitimate traffic hit, a net loss that imposes the cost of
mitigation without its benefit. A more concentrated benign
population ($\alpha = 2.0$) raises that collateral to $61.1\%$.

Enrichment removes the failure, blocking $90.0\%$, $90.3\%$ and $85.0\%$ of the
attack at $M = 5$, $25$ and $100$ stacks, in every case with \textbf{no collateral
observed} across the $n = 15$ campaigns, and reproducing the frequency rule where
it worked. The surviving $10$--$15\%$ is the tail of attackers with
one-off fingerprints: scoped mitigation trades completeness for precision.

\textbf{Two conditions bound the result.} The first is the boundary of the
threat model: when the
botnet adopts common benign fingerprints, little is enriched and the rule blocks
$30.4\%$ of the attack at $3.78\%$ collateral, against $3.6\%$ and $39.0\%$ for the
frequency rule. The selectivity advantage is largely lost, but it is lost
\textit{safely}: with a stricter $\rho$ the scope declines to name a fingerprint and
degenerates to the global control, the correct report when no discriminator
exists. The
second is the background profile. Moderate drift is tolerable ($\alpha = 2.0$
profile against an $\alpha = 1.5$ episode: $90.3\%$ coverage, $0.45\%$ collateral).
A flat or missing profile is not, since every fingerprint then looks rare, the
benign head scores as enriched, and collateral jumps to $81.2\%$. Profile
quality governs precision and leaves coverage unaffected, so keeping the
profile fresh is a deployment requirement. It also implies a fail-safe: where the
profile fails a freshness or drift check, scoped mitigation is suppressed and the
verdict emitted as evidence only, as the enrichment test does when nothing
is enriched.

\subsection{Cost and the Choice of $W$}
\label{sec:latency}

Appendix~\ref{app:cost} measures both paths against window occupancy. Kept as
classes, the equality-based sub-relations make admission constant at about
$0.37\,\mu$s per session and the symbolic layer linear, at
$26.4$~s for $100\,000$ sessions, against $52.8$~s for
pair edges at $1\,000$. $\Omega(S)$ still grows with the square of the cluster,
since it counts pairs. Computing it no longer does. Detection is flat over a
$30\times$ range of $W$ (Table~\ref{tab:window}), so $W$ can be as small as the
traffic permits.

\subsection{Statistical Significance}

Paired Wilcoxon tests over the $n = 30$ runs, with Bonferroni correction and
Cohen's $d$, make both contrasts significant in the distributed regime
($K = 1000$) at $p_{\mathrm{Bonf}} = 7.5\times 10^{-9}$: \textbf{(d)$-$(c)} with
$d = 13.5$ and \textbf{(d)$-$(a)} with $d = 22.4$. The matched-pairs rank-biserial
correlation is $+1.00$ for both, (d) winning all 30 paired runs. Seeds are draws
from one generator, so the tests show that the difference is stable across draws
of that process, and effect sizes of this magnitude reflect the near-perfect
separation of controlled synthetic traffic. Neither should be read as an
expectation of field performance.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

We first place the framework in operation and discuss how it partitions, then
state the five limitations that bound our claims. Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}
lists further ones.

\textbf{Operational placement.} The framework closes a management loop:
detection, evidence and scope come from one pass over the
same graph, so what reaches the operator or a SOAR playbook is a STIX
\textit{course-of-action} already parameterized by the discriminator that justified
the verdict.

\textbf{Parallelism and partitioning.} The rule dictates the natural shard key.
Condition (ii) requires every session of $S$ to target one endpoint, so
partitioning the window by endpoint evaluates the rule \textit{exactly}, with no
cross-shard traffic and per-shard state bounded by the window. The price is
that a campaign spreading one JA4 across many endpoints accumulates too few pairs
in any shard to clear $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$, while sharding by JA4 breaks the
endpoint condition. No single key preserves all six sub-relations, and
Appendix~\ref{app:cost} sketches a two-tier design.

\textbf{The gain is regime-specific.} Against single-source floods, per-IP or
per-session thresholds perform comparably, and on the conventional real attacks of
public datasets a strong per-session classifier already reaches
$\mathrm{AUC} \geq 0.98$. The advantage holds only when the campaign is
distributed \textit{and} stealthy, and the framework complements volumetric
defenses.

\textbf{The synthetic evaluation demonstrates a mechanism, and does not
establish field performance.} In the synthetic setting the campaign carries
exactly the coordination signals configuration (d) measures, and attacker
sessions draw their per-session attributes from real benign distributions. The
per-session non-separability of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} is therefore no artifact
of a weakened baseline. The mimicry, however, is imposed by construction, so the
result shows the mechanism under perfect per-session indistinguishability and
establishes no performance on a real capture of that regime.

\textbf{Detection and scoping both depend on an observable high-weight
discriminator.} A robustness sweep ($n = 10$) makes the dependency explicit: as
the fraction of origins sharing the TLS fingerprint falls, $\mathrm{AUC}$(d)
decreases monotonically from $1.00$ to $\approx 0.74$ under randomization, a
residual left by the finite benign JA4 pool, which internet-scale diversity should
drive to chance. Endpoint convergence does \textit{not} compensate, because
legitimate users converge on the endpoint too. The weighted family tolerates
partial breakage, since surviving high-weight signals carry the cluster, but a bot
outside the threat model that presents a browser's JA4 while jittering timing and
rotating identities degrades it toward chance. Encrypted Client
Hello~\cite{ietf2024ech} could also make JA4 unobservable.

\textbf{Scope of the ablation.} Configuration (d)
expresses the graph's evidence as features: a
linear model over the same three cross-session features still attains $0.799$
against $0.495$ for the per-session set, so the AUC gain in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} comes from representing cross-session structure, and
OWL adds nothing to that number. What OWL adds is operational. The verdict, its
decomposition per sub-relation and the scope form one derivation, exported to
STIX without translation (Listing~\ref{lst:chain}), and a new signal enters as
one weighted sub-property that the aggregation reads from the ontology. We call
the verdict \textit{explainable} in this sense of an inspectable derivation.
Whether it shortens an analyst's decision is untested. Table~\ref{tab:symbolic} supports
the symbolic path only in part: it trails the learned model against a monolithic
botnet, and pulls ahead once fragmentation makes the score distribution unusable
for a zero-false-positive threshold.

\textbf{Attack-family, protocol and dataset coverage.} The ontology and the
evaluation cover the Slow HTTP DoS family over HTTP/1.1, and HTTP/2 or other
protocols need further sub-relations. Real-data validation spans six
laboratory attacks in two datasets, and the single-attack comparison with
KLAGE differs in granularity and protocol, so a superiority claim requires
reproducing KLAGE under our protocol.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We modeled the HTTP session as a first-class entity, related to other sessions by
six typed sub-properties weighted by evasion cost, and fired a SPARQL/SWRL rule
whose derivation serves as verdict, evidence chain and mitigation scope.

Against stealthy campaigns from botnets of compromised devices, per-session
detection sits at chance across four classifier families while the cross-session
representation reaches ROC AUC $0.98$, and on conventional real attacks a strong
per-session classifier suffices. Scoping by the property most of the cluster
shares selects a legitimate fingerprint once the botnet spans several TLS stacks,
and enrichment over a profile of normal traffic restores $85$--$90\%$ coverage
with no observed collateral. As a detector, the symbolic path recovers $90\%$ of
a 25-stack campaign where the learned model recovers $36\%$ at zero false
positives, a gap the AUC hides. Immediate directions are HTTP/2 attacks, a
semi-synthetic evaluation over production background traffic, and a captured
stealthy campaign, which no benchmark yet provides.

\section{Runtime Construction and Sub-Relation Instantiation}
\label{app:algorithms}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Runtime knowledge-graph construction}
\label{alg:kg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE request stream $\mathcal{R}$, ontology $\mathcal{O}$, window $W$
\ENSURE knowledge graph $KG$ persistent over $W$
\STATE $KG \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FOR{each request $r \in \mathcal{R}$}
  \STATE $s,i,e \leftarrow \textsc{ResolveSession/Identity/Endpoint}(r)$
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{add}(s,i,e)$; \, $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{hasIdentity},i)$
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{targets},e)$
  \FOR{each active $s' \in S_W$, $s' \neq s$}
    \FOR{each sub-relation $i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}$}
      \IF{$\textsc{Holds}_i(s,s')$}
        \STATE $KG.\mathrm{link}(s,\texttt{relatedBy}_i,s')$
      \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE $KG.\mathrm{purgeOutside}(W)$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $KG$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:kg} constructs the graph over the sliding window. For each
pair $(s_a,s_b)$ active in $W$, the following procedures decide whether the edge
is instantiated.

\textbf{\texttt{TLSFingerprint}.} Two-stage match: exact equality of the full
JA4 or, failing that, near variants (same transport/TLS-version/ALPN
prefix, at most one symbol of difference in the ordered cipher or extension
blocks), instantiated with a \textit{ja4\_distance = 1} annotation. This absorbs
version drift within one client library without admitting spurious matches
across distinct stacks. Cost $O(1)$ per pair.

\textbf{\texttt{ReusedIdentity}.} Instantiated on any non-empty overlap between
the observed identity sets, $(\mathrm{cookie}(s_a) \cap \mathrm{cookie}(s_b))
\cup (\mathrm{token}(s_a) \cap \mathrm{token}(s_b)) \cup (\mathrm{user}(s_a) \cap
\mathrm{user}(s_b)) \neq \emptyset$. JWTs are decoded and compared by the
\texttt{sub} claim; cookies and usernames are normalized (trim, lower-case).
Cost $O(1)$ amortized with an inverted index.

\textbf{\texttt{TemporalPattern}.} With $\Delta(s)$ the sequence of
inter-arrival times, we compute
$d_{\mathrm{DTW}}(s_a,s_b) = \mathrm{DTW}(\Delta(s_a),\Delta(s_b)) /
\max(|\Delta(s_a)|,|\Delta(s_b)|)$ and instantiate when
$d_{\mathrm{DTW}} \leq \tau_{\mathrm{DTW}}$. Sessions with fewer than three
requests are skipped. FastDTW under a constant-width Sakoe--Chiba band reduces
the per-pair cost from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$, and locality-sensitive hashing over
summary vectors ($\bar\delta$, $\sigma_\delta$, $\delta_{\min}$,
$\delta_{\max}$, entropy) avoids all-pairs comparison.

\textbf{\texttt{PayloadSignature}.} With
$\mathbf{p}(s) = (\overline{\mathrm{len}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{len}}, h_{\mathrm{UA}},
h_{\mathrm{CT}})$, the mean and standard deviation of body size plus hashes of
the dominant User-Agent and Content-Type, the edge is instantiated when
cosine similarity exceeds $\tau_{\mathrm{payload}}$. Cost $O(1)$ given
precomputed vectors.

\textbf{\texttt{EndpointConvergence}.} Instantiated when both sessions converge
on the same endpoint, under path-pattern normalization
(\texttt{/api/users/12345} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{/api/users/\{id\}}) by default;
an additional exact-path match annotates the edge with \textit{exact = true}. Cost
$O(1)$ with a prefix-tree index.

\textbf{\texttt{NetworkProximity}.} Instantiated on a shared IPv4 /24 (or IPv6
/48) prefix, or a shared ASN, or both; when both hold the edge is annotated
\textit{strong = true}. ASN resolution uses current BGP tables. Cost $O(1)$ with
an inverted prefix index.

\section{Weight Calibration}
\label{app:calibration}

Empirical calibration depends critically on the realism of the traffic. On
laboratory datasets, with a few dozen distinct JA4 fingerprints, optimization
even inverts the sign of the TLS signal, because benign sessions share
fingerprints more than partially coordinated campaigns do; this is a testbed
artifact of low JA4 diversity, absent from the real internet.

In the realistic scenario (thousands of legitimate JA4 fingerprints, users
hitting the attacked service), we calibrate by maximizing per-session
attack-vs-benign ROC AUC over the score
$w_{\mathrm{tls}}\,z(\mathrm{ja4}) + w_{\mathrm{ep}}\,z(\mathrm{endpoint\ conv.})
+ w_{\mathrm{net}}\,z(\mathrm{net\ prox.})$ with standardized attributes, over
the grid $\{0.3\ldots1.0\}^3$ (125 combinations, 60 scenarios, 91\,500
sessions). The result corroborates the proposed ordering: the best vector is
$(w_{\mathrm{tls}}{=}1.0,\ w_{\mathrm{ep}}{=}0.3,\ w_{\mathrm{net}}{=}0.3)$, and
the TLS fingerprint is the only individually discriminative signal (isolated
$\mathrm{AUC} = 0.93$, against $0.50$ for endpoint convergence and $0.58$ for
network proximity). The weights of Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology}
$(1.0,\ 0.6,\ 0.3)$ attain the same optimal $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.943$ and are
insensitive to $\pm20\%$ perturbation. Calibration
therefore validates the \textit{ordering}; in the pure same-service regime the
medium and low weights are not separately identifiable, since both
sit near chance, and their absolute values remain open pending production
traffic with partial and conflicting signals.

\subsection*{Generator Realism}

Two caveats apply to the production measurement of benign fingerprint popularity.
The log carries no connection identifier, so weighting is per request, not
per session, which overweights heavy clients and if anything \textit{overstates}
concentration; and the sample is one point of presence over one window. The
CICIDS2017 testbed~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} is more concentrated still
($52.7\%$ head), so laboratory shape is indicative only. The measured curve is
also not exactly Zipf: its head matches $\alpha = 1.5$ ($38.9\%$ against
$38.4\%$) while its top ten matches $\alpha = 2.0$ ($94.2\%$ against $93.8\%$),
so the sweep brackets it.

\section{Additional Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\textbf{Session and identity instrumentation.} The approach assumes sessions,
identities and TLS fingerprints can be extracted from traffic events; where that
instrumentation is partial or noisy, performance degrades.

\textbf{Graph maintenance cost.} The symbolic figures of
Appendix~\ref{app:cost} are an upper bound, for the reason given there, and
scaling to CDN traffic requires the partitioning of Section~\ref{sec:discussion},
which we did not implement.

\textbf{Protocol coverage.} The current ontology covers the Slow HTTP DoS family
over HTTP/1.1. HTTP/2 attacks and other application
protocols (DNS, gRPC) need additional sub-relations.

\textbf{Sub-relation coverage.} The evaluated $\Omega(S)$ instantiates three of the
six sub-relations (TLS fingerprint, endpoint convergence, network proximity),
the ones the generator and public captures expose. Identity reuse, temporal
pattern and payload signature are specified in Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms} but
not exercised, so the weights of Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology} hold only for those
three.

\textbf{Dataset breadth.} Real-data validation covers one dataset and one attack
for the head-to-head comparison, plus six attacks across CICIDS2017 and
CIC-IoT2023 for the regime analysis. RT-IoT2022, CIC-DDoS2019 and anonymized
production traffic remain future work. The gap persists in newer benchmarks:
BCCC-cPacket-Cloud-DDoS-2024~\cite{shafi2024bccc} identifies inadequate
application-layer attack representation as a dataset deficiency, yet its 17 DDoS
scenarios are all TCP-based. Among the benchmarks examined we found no
independent capture of the stealthy Layer-7 regime studied here.

\textbf{Explanation validation.} Evidence chains pass the automated completeness
and actionability checks of Section~\ref{sec:method} ($7/7$). We ran no user
study, so whether they shorten an analyst's time to decision is untested.

\section{Cost Model and Window Sensitivity}
\label{app:cost}

\begin{figure}[tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig5_latency.png}
\caption{Cost of both layers, with pair edges and with class counts.}
\label{fig:latency}
\end{figure}

Admission happens per request while materialization and the $\Omega$
aggregation run once per window, so we time the two layers separately, on a
synthetic window in the realistic mix (30\% of sessions in one campaign sharing a
botnet JA4, benign JA4 from a pool of 2\,000, eight endpoints, dispersed /24s),
with medians over three repeats on one core. Each layer runs in two ways: with
pair edges, as the rules are written, and with class counts for the
equality-based sub-relations. Both give the same $\Omega(S)$ for every endpoint,
to within $7\times 10^{-12}$. This is a cost model of the algorithm, not a system
benchmark: the symbolic layer runs on in-memory \texttt{rdflib}, not the
Jena/TDB2 backend, so its figures are an upper bound.

With pair enumeration, median admission latency grows from
$2.3\,\mu$s at $|S_W| = 100$ to $127\,\mu$s at $|S_W| = 10\,000$,
tracking the candidate-pair count (148 to 6\,495 edges per admission) at
$15$--$21$~ns per pair. With class counters it stays between
$0.33$ and $0.42\,\mu$s from $100$ to $100\,000$ sessions
(Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}, where dotted lines are reference slopes).

Materializing pair edges costs $0.84$~s at $|S_W| = 100$ and
$52.8$~s at $1\,000$, within $198$--$309\,\mu$s per RDF edge,
so that path is linear in edges and quadratic in sessions. Class aggregation
materializes one membership triple per session and sub-relation, and computes
$\Omega(S)$ with the query of Listing~\ref{lst:rules} in $0.33$~s at $1\,000$
sessions, $161$ times faster, and $26.4$~s at $100\,000$. The
quadratic term thus lives in the value of $\Omega(S)$, which counts pairs, and in
the pair edges of the non-transitive sub-relations, whose candidates the indexes
restrict. The evidence chain needs only the pair counts
(Listing~\ref{lst:chain}), which the classes give directly.

\subsection*{Partitioning at CDN Scale}

The design that endpoint sharding suggests, unimplemented here, is two-tiered:
endpoint-sharded workers plus a global sketch over high-weight identifiers to
recover campaigns that spread across endpoints. Quantifying the recall lost by
the first tier and recovered by the second is a precondition for CDN-scale
deployment. With the cost model above, endpoint sharding is exact and needs no
coordination, class counting keeps both layers linear in the window, and
admission takes well under a microsecond. Confirming this at CDN scale remains
future work.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The reference implementation is available at
\url{https://github.com/Natanael0liveira/knowledge-graph-ddos-article} under a
permissive license. It is organized by experimental stage: the extraction
pipeline (PCAP $\rightarrow$ JA4 and flows $\rightarrow$ sessions $\rightarrow$
graph); the calibrated generator, with the realism parameters of
Section~\ref{sec:method} and its canonical and adversarial configurations; the
ablation, the academic baselines, the four classifier families and the
statistical runner; the symbolic layer, SWRL rules plus the SPARQL aggregation
with weights read from the ontology; and the evidence and mitigation modules.

Both scope derivations ship side by side: the frequency rule that fails and the
enrichment rule that replaces it, so the negative result of
Section~\ref{sec:collateral} is reproducible rather than asserted. The
background profile the enrichment test needs is produced by an attack-free
generator run, so no labels enter the decision path at any point.

Seeds are fixed, orchestration is driven by Makefiles, and artifacts are
versioned, so every table and data figure regenerates from a single command per
stage. Fig.~\ref{fig:ontology} is a schematic; its source ships with the
artifact. The OWL ontology is provided in RDF/XML, with
every \texttt{relatedBy} sub-property annotated with its
\texttt{coordinationWeight}. We intend to submit these artifacts to the Artifact
Evaluation track.
