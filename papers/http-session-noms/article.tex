Luigi Carro, J\'eferson Campos Nobre, and Eder John Scheid}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Institute of Informatics, Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS), Porto Alegre, Brazil\\
\IEEEauthorrefmark{1}Also with Azion Technologies, Brazil\\
\texttt{\{natanael.oliveira, arthur.kobielski, marcos.luna, carro, jcnobre, ejscheid\}@inf.ufrgs.br}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Low-rate distributed application-layer DDoS attacks keep
every source below per-source thresholds, leaving operators
with a critical decision:
which clients to block without harming legitimate traffic.
We address this decision through a calibrated statistical test
that selects TLS fingerprints over-represented relative to an
endpoint's normal traffic. We evaluate the approach on generated
campaigns and 23 days of traffic from four CDN endpoints.
In generated campaigns, the alarm's most common fingerprint
blocks $39\%$ of legitimate traffic and no attackers once
the
botnet spans five TLS stacks. Profile-relative scoping
covers $90\%$ of attackers on up to 25 new stacks, with no
observed collateral. On production traffic, the volume
trigger and scope together misfire in $0.1\%$ of attack-free
windows on the days used for configuration (though each
alone exceeds the $1\%$ budget) and in $0.19\%$ over 14
later pre-specified days, about twice that rate but within
budget.
These rare misfires still block a median $33$--$39\%$ of
their windows' clients. Legitimate client fleets raise a
calibration floor, the botnet size needed to select rare
shared
fingerprints. A botnet on common fingerprints mostly
escapes. On the busiest endpoint,
the volume trigger misses most of a small injected botnet.
An OWL ontology specifies the evidence supporting mitigation
decisions and enables compilation of the corresponding queries.
Our findings characterize when TLS-fingerprint scoping provides
selective mitigation and when rate limits or challenges are needed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Application-layer Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS)
attacks can overwhelm services through different traffic
patterns. High-volume attacks, such as HTTP/2 Rapid Reset
(CVE-2023-44487), have reached peaks of 398 million
requests per second~\cite{google2023rapidreset}. Low-rate
distributed attacks instead spread traffic across many
origins, keeping individual sources below rate thresholds.
Distributed Slow HTTP DoS~\cite{cambiaso2013slowdos} and
flooding tools that hold connections open, such as HULK and
GoldenEye~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids}, can exhaust a server
this way, without high per-source rates.

A stealthy campaign makes each session look legitimate on
the per-session signals that detectors use, such as
connection duration and packet rate~\cite{kemp2023approach}.
TLS fingerprints group clients by their handshakes, so
traffic from different origins can be compared.
Legitimate clients, however, often share them.
What matters is whether a fingerprint appears more often
than an endpoint's normal traffic predicts. Choosing which
clients to block is thus a management decision that needs
evidence limiting harm to legitimate users. Even a low
false-alarm
rate may hide substantial collateral damage.

Existing detectors at most flag an attack, leaving the
choice of whom to block open.
A survey of 75 application-layer DDoS studies found that
$47\%$ use request-stream and packet-pattern
features~\cite{odusami2020survey}. Graph-based detectors
capture relations between network nodes but stop at a
verdict~\cite{belcastro2025klage}. Aggregate-based
mitigations use network and transport
headers~\cite{mahajan2002pushback,alcoz2022accturbo}, while
source-based filtering balances attack coverage against
legitimate traffic~\cite{soldo2012filtering}. Standard
mitigation channels filter on the same
fields~\cite{dots2020data,rfc8955flowspec}. These approaches
leave open when a TLS-fingerprint filter can stop an attack
without excessive collateral, and when it cannot.

We study this decision using a calibrated statistical
test (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). For each endpoint and
five-minute window, a volume trigger first raises an alarm.
The test then selects the \textit{scope}, the TLS
fingerprints to block, by how often they appear among the
alarm's origins relative to a \textit{profile} of the
endpoint's normal traffic.
We consider botnets of compromised devices, which we assume
keep their own TLS stacks. We also
evaluate attacks using common fingerprints to identify
the method's limits.

This paper makes three contributions. (i) \emph{Profile-relative
scoping.} We select fingerprints that occur more often
than expected from normal traffic. On generated campaigns,
this blocks most attackers without labels, while the alarm's
most common fingerprint blocks legitimate clients and no
attackers once the botnet spans several stacks. (ii)
\emph{Statistical calibration.}
We calibrate the test per endpoint to a $1\%$ false-alarm
budget on attack-free windows. This sets a
\textit{calibration floor}, the botnet size from which the
scope selects a typical stack. We measure the floor and
false-alarm rate on eight days of four CDN endpoints, and
the rate on 15 later days. (iii) \emph{A session
ontology.} An OWL ontology defines the evidence the test
reads and the selected filter. Log-store queries are
compiled from it, so a new equality signal needs no change
to query-generation code. The exchange standards we examined
do not express
this JA4 filter in their core vocabulary.

\section{Related Work}

Table~\ref{tab:related} compares prior approaches by
the traffic they select, how filters are chosen, and
their effects on legitimate users.

\textit{Scoping a mitigation.}
Aggregate-based defenses group traffic by packet-header
fields and rate-limit each
group~\cite{mahajan2002pushback,alcoz2022accturbo}.
Blackholing drops all traffic to attacked destinations,
with finer header-based rules reducing the affected
traffic~\cite{dietzel2018stellar}.
Source-based filtering selects prefixes under a
filter budget, balancing attack coverage against
legitimate traffic~\cite{soldo2012filtering}.
These approaches show how filter granularity affects
collateral, but none selects clients by TLS fingerprint.
Closer to our method, anomaly extraction isolates feature
values over-represented in abnormal
traffic~\cite{silveira2010urca,brauckhoff2009extraction},
and signature generation checks candidate signatures against
normal traffic~\cite{li2006hamsa}. Our calibrated test asks
the same question of TLS fingerprints, relative to each
endpoint's normal traffic. Other
defenses use client
puzzles~\cite{kandula2005botz} or schedule requests by their
deviation from normal workloads~\cite{ranjan2009ddosshield},
imposing costs on
legitimate users without selecting fingerprint filters.
Mitigation signaling mechanisms such as the IETF DOTS
data channel~\cite{dots2020data} and BGP Flow
Specification~\cite{rfc8955flowspec} carry filters on
network and transport fields, and their core rules cannot
express JA4.

\textit{Layer-7 detection.}
Application-layer DDoS detectors use traffic profiles
and session features~\cite{tripathi2021application},
including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) over flow
statistics~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous}, behavioral
representations of sessions~\cite{bharathi2012pca},
and learned models~\cite{kemp2023approach}.
None of these preserves relations across sessions, and a
verdict does not say which fingerprint filter to apply or
how many legitimate clients it would block. Other work
separates flash crowds from attacks by request patterns
across a website~\cite{xie2009monitoring}.

\textit{Knowledge graphs and fingerprints.}
Security knowledge graphs represent threats and
countermeasures, including D3FEND's OWL-based
model~\cite{kaloroumakis2021d3fend}, while their operational
use remains unclear~\cite{liu2022recent}.
Traffic-derived graphs also support detection:
KLAGE~\cite{belcastro2025klage} classifies nodes in graphs
built from network logs.
Passive TLS fingerprinting identifies client software from
its handshake~\cite{husak2016https}, although applications
built on one library often share a
fingerprint~\cite{anderson2019tls}.
This sharing enables fingerprint filtering and also creates
collateral risk.
Most bots that claim a browser identity expose TLS and
header fingerprints of another tool~\cite{li2021goodbot}.
One bot-management platform already aggregates JA4 signals
across requests~\cite{martinetti2024ja4signals},
but its published description specifies no calibrated filter
selection or collateral metric.
Our ontology specifies the evidence and selected
scope, while the statistical test determines
which fingerprints to block.

\section{Calibrated Fingerprint Scoping}
\label{sec:approach}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1_scoping.png}
\caption{Calibrated fingerprint scoping. The query compiled from the session ontology returns, per endpoint and window, the class sizes read by the trigger and the scope, and every threshold and level comes from attack-free days.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

This section defines the threat model, trigger, scope,
calibration floor, and ontology specification
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

The defender operates the targeted application and terminates its TLS.
For each request, it records the TLS \textit{ClientHello},
source address with prefix and ASN, target endpoint,
arrival time, and any application identity.
From the \textit{ClientHello}, it computes a JA4
fingerprint~\cite{althouse2023ja4}, which summarizes
the TLS version, cipher suites, extensions and ALPN.
It sees neither the botnet's control channel nor which
sources are coordinated, but can profile normal traffic
outside attack episodes.

The attacker controls $K$ compromised devices and
may make each session statistically indistinguishable
from a legitimate one, the stealth assumption of our
evaluation. Each session holds a connection and worker as
legitimate traffic does, so no per-session rate rises. We
assume
a bot keeps the fingerprint of its TLS stack, the library
shipped with its device or toolkit. Botnets span many
router, camera and DVR models~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}.
Imitating a browser's \textit{ClientHello} takes a full
browser or an impersonation library kept in step with
browser releases~\cite{frolov2019tls}. Browser-imitating
bots fall outside our threat model, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} tests such attacks as its
boundary.

\subsection{The Trigger}
\label{sec:rule}

For each endpoint $e$ and five-minute window, let $S$ be the
sessions to $e$ and $n$ their distinct origins,
counting each address once per fingerprint it presents.
The production trigger fires when $n \geq k_{\min}=5$
and $n$ reaches its 99th percentile over attack-free
windows. We call this the \textbf{distinct-origin gate}.

The \textbf{base rule}, which the held-out protocol takes as
reference, requires the coordination
mass $\Omega(S)$ of the ontology's
\texttt{CoordinatedHTTPFlood} class to reach
$\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ at the same percentile. $\Omega$
counts weighted pairs of distinct
origins sharing a JA4, endpoint or /24 prefix
(Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}). Its endpoint term,
$0.6\binom{n}{2}$, gives $91\%$ of the score in the default
campaign (Listing~\ref{lst:chain}, Appendix~\ref{app:cost}),
so $\Omega$ mostly measures volume. The scope is what sets
the attack apart.

\subsection{The Scope and Its Calibration}
\label{sec:evidence}

The scope determines which clients a mitigation would
affect. Selecting the alarm's most common (modal)
fingerprint is unreliable because it may belong to
legitimate users. Consider a botnet of $A$ attackers spread
uniformly over $M$ stacks. About $0.9A/M$ attackers use each
stack, while
the remaining tenth use one-off fingerprints. If the most
common legitimate fingerprint holds a share
$p_1$ of $n_b$ legitimate origins, the modal fingerprint
belongs to the attack only while $0.9A/M > p_1n_b$.
With the measured $p_1=38.4\%$ of requests
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), used as a proxy for origins,
and a campaign as large as legitimate traffic, this
holds only for $M<2.3$. With more stacks, the modal filter
blocks legitimate clients and no attackers.

We therefore compare the alarm with a \textbf{profile} of
the endpoint's normal traffic, collected outside attack
episodes.
Let $b(f)$ be the share of fingerprint $f$ among the
profile's $N$ pooled origin--fingerprint pairs, plus $1/N$
so that no background is zero. Let
$c(f)$ count the alarm's origins presenting $f$, and
$n=\sum_f c(f)$. The \textbf{binomial enrichment test}
selects every $f$ that is both enriched, $c(f)/n \geq \rho
b(f)$ with $\rho=3$, and unlikely at level $\lambda_e$,
$\Pr[X\geq c(f)]<\lambda_e/|F|$ for
$X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,b(f))$. The Bonferroni divisor
$|F|$ counts fingerprints in the profile and alarm.
The test selects a \textit{set} of fingerprints, so it can cover a
botnet spread over several stacks.

The \textbf{$z$-score} compares each $c(f)$ with its
binomial background. The uncalibrated \textbf{unseen} filter
selects fingerprints absent from the profile and seen in at
least $k_{\min}$ origins. Its \textbf{union} with the test
adds new stacks too small for the level.

On generated traffic, each origin carries one session
and $\lambda_e=0.01$. Production traffic differs:
legitimate client \textit{fleets} can become active
together, violating the binomial independence assumption.
We therefore calibrate $\lambda_e$ per endpoint as the
largest value up to $0.01$ at which the scope selects a
filter in at most $1\%$ of attack-free
calibration windows, our \textit{false-alarm budget}.
At levels such as $10^{-60}$, the binomial tail is no longer
a reliable probability and serves as a score calibrated on
observed traffic.
The $z$-score threshold, the 99th percentile of each
window's largest score and never below 3, meets the same
budget. Both scopes satisfy
the budget on calibration windows by construction.
Section~\ref{sec:production} evaluates later days.

The profile requires no attack labels, but must exclude
attack episodes. Otherwise, a campaign spanning the
profile would no longer appear enriched. Conversely,
attackers using a common legitimate fingerprint may
not enrich it enough to be selected. The scope then cannot
tell them from normal clients.

\subsection{The Calibration Floor}
\label{sec:floor}

Calibration limits false alarms but also raises the minimum
attack size needed for selection. We call this the
\textbf{calibration floor}: the botnet size from which the
scope selects a typical stack.
For a stack with $c$ attackers among $n$ origins, the test
requires $c/n\geq\rho b(f)$ and
$\Pr[X\geq c]<\lambda_e/|F|$.
The floor is the smallest $A$ for which a stack of $0.9A/M$
attackers passes both in a window of $n_0+A$ origins, where
$n_0$ is the
median calibration window. Stricter levels raise
this floor.

In the union, the unseen filter selects a stack absent from the
profile once it has $k_{\min}$ origins, requiring $\lceil
k_{\min}M/0.9\rceil$ attackers at any level, 139 for $M=25$.
Shared fingerprints need the calibrated test, and there
$\rho$ sets an \textit{enrichment limit}: a stack's expected
share stays below $0.9/M$, so a fingerprint whose background
share exceeds $0.9/(\rho M)$, $1.2\%$ for $M=25$ and
$\rho=3$, is not expected to be selected.

We evaluate two ways to lower the floor.
First, \textit{known fleets} are fingerprints selected
at level $0.01$ in at least $5\%$ of calibration
windows. Exempting them from both the scope and
calibration acts as an allow-list on the busiest
endpoint and the API. Together, these fingerprints carry up
to about $7\%$ of their profiles, and the scope never blocks
attackers using them.

Second, a \textbf{beta-binomial background}, developed
after the held-out day was examined
(Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}), models correlation
within windows. Per profile fingerprint, its correlation
$\phi(f)$ is estimated by moments over calibration windows
from
$\mathrm{Var}[c(f)]=nb(1-b)(1+(n-1)\phi)$,
with $\phi$ clipped to $[0,0.99]$.
Counts are then tested against a beta-binomial
with the same mean. Fingerprints absent from the profile stay binomial.

\subsection{The Session Ontology as Specification}
\label{sec:ontology}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The ontology specifies the counts read by the trigger and
scope, and is what an operator would deploy. Its central
class, \texttt{ApplicationSession},
links each session to an \texttt{Identity}, source
\texttt{IPAddress}, and \texttt{Endpoint}.
An \texttt{Attack} links to its \texttt{Mitigation},
whose evidence chain records the selected scope.
Sub-properties of a symmetric \texttt{relatedTo}
describe relationships between sessions
(Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}).

The three relations used here are equalities (shared JA4,
endpoint or /24 prefix) that partition sessions into
classes. Each is annotated with the property it compares,
and \texttt{relatedTo} with the unit it counts.
From these annotations and a property-to-column binding, we
compile SQL queries that return the class sizes read by the
gate, base rule and scope per endpoint and window.
No reasoner runs in evaluation.

A new equality signal requires four triples and
a binding for any new session property, without
changes to the query-generation code. When the rule fires,
the evidence chain (rule, cluster, scope and the split of
$\Omega(S)$ by sub-relation) is exported as JSON-LD
(Listing~\ref{lst:chain}, Appendix~\ref{app:cost})
and STIX~2.1~\cite{oasis2021stix}, using an
\textit{indicator} and a \textit{course-of-action}
linked by \textit{mitigates}.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Three traffic sources answer different questions.
A generator, calibrated on benign CICIDS2017 sessions and
JA4 fingerprints measured at a CDN, runs controlled
campaigns. Laboratory captures test conventional attacks
and provide an uncontrolled comparison with the published
results of KLAGE, the closest system
(Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). The CDN's traffic over 23
days measures false alarms and, with injected botnets,
coverage.

\subsection{Scenarios and Datasets}

\textbf{Controlled evaluation: calibrated synthetic traffic.}
A generator produces distributed Slow HTTP DoS campaigns
against one endpoint with known ground truth. Its main
parameter is the \textit{degree of distribution} $K$, the
number of devices controlled by the attacker. We report $K =
50$, a small botnet whose origins each stay below their own
rate limit, and $K = 1000$, spread across hundreds of /24
prefixes, with almost no per-session signal. Legitimate
fingerprint popularity follows a
Zipf distribution with exponent $\alpha$, as real client
populations are concentrated.
We compare this distribution with $6.33$M TLS requests
recorded at a production edge point of presence of
Azion Technologies\footnote{Measured August 22, 2026,
from an existing platform access log. The extract holds only
client-software fingerprints and their frequencies, with no
address, host, URI, header or locale.}: $495$ distinct
fingerprints,
the most common carrying $38.4\%$ of requests and the top
ten $93.8\%$. The measured head is consistent with $\alpha =
1.5$, the top ten closer to $\alpha = 2.0$, and we use
$\alpha = 1.5$ by default.

No published measurement shows how botnets are distributed
across TLS stacks, so we vary it.
We spread $90\%$ of attackers uniformly at random over $M$
stacks, the hardest case in expectation for selecting a
stack at a given $M$. The rest use one-off
fingerprints, and $M = 25$ by default.
In \textit{shared} mode, stacks come from the profile's
tail, which legitimate clients also present.
In \textit{adversarial} mode, attackers use the most
common legitimate fingerprints, as browser-imitating
bots might.

Legitimate request counts and durations are sampled
from benign CICIDS2017 sessions
(Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}). Kolmogorov--Smirnov
distances of $D = 0.002$ and $0.003$ between generated
and source sessions therefore check only the sampler. In
\textit{stealth}
mode, attacker sessions use the same distributions,
leaving only cross-session structure to distinguish
the campaign. Attackers start within the first 30~s,
accounting for a median $34$--$45\%$ of origins in
overlapping windows and $50\%$ of the alarm's
\textit{cluster}. The cluster chains sessions of
an endpoint while consecutive starts remain within
300~s of one another.

\textbf{Production traffic.}
We use eight initial days from four endpoints of Azion's own
services: real-user monitoring beacons (E1),
a web console (E2), an API (E3), and single sign-on
(E4). Their median windows hold about $3\,000$, 62, 41 and 20 origins.
Of 12 exported endpoints, these are the four with TLS, at
least 50 calibration windows and a median window of at least
$k_{\min}$ origins. Exports contain distinct origins and
connections per fingerprint and window, together
with same-/24 pairs. The scope is evaluated offline
behind the operator's WAF, so profiles and floors describe
the clients the WAF lets through.
Calibration days are assumed attack-free: an attack there
would lower the test level and
raise the gate threshold, making both more cautious.
Pre-injection test windows are also treated as
\textit{clean}, so a real attack there would count as a
false alarm.

Each of the last five initial days is tested with the
profile, thresholds and levels of earlier days. We inject
generated botnets
into each window, with 25 to $1\,000$ attackers,
or from a tenth of the median window (\textit{tenth-size}) to all of it.
Of these attackers, $90\%$ are assigned uniformly at random
to 25 stacks and the rest to one-off fingerprints that no
scope selects, so at most about $90\%$ can be blocked.
Stacks are absent from the traffic (\textit{new}), drawn
from the profile's tail beyond its ten most common
fingerprints (\textit{shared}), or the endpoint's 25 most
common ones (\textit{adversarial}). A flash crowd adds 100
or $1\,000$ of the day's clients to one window. A ninth day,
the \textit{held-out day}, and 14 more, the \textit{block},
were analyzed as fixed in advance.

\subsection{Baselines, Configurations and Metrics}
\label{sec:configs}

On generated traffic, we compare four scopes
applied to the alarm's cluster: the modal
fingerprint, unseen filter, $z$-score ($z > 3$),
and binomial test. The learned baseline is a
Random Forest trained on campaign labels using
cross-session evidence from the three sub-relations
(configuration (d), Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).
We constrain it to zero false positives, which the test also
achieves wherever the
learned model is evaluated, except in the
adversarial case. On production traffic, we evaluate the
configurations in Table~\ref{tab:configs}, with thresholds,
levels and known fleets estimated from attack-free days.
\textit{In sample}, each calibration day is judged against a
profile that includes its own traffic, so its fleets look
less enriched. \textit{Cross-fitting}, introduced post hoc,
judges each calibration day, like a test day, against a
profile of the other days.

Five choices were made on the test days:
counting origins, calibrating $\lambda_e$,
the gate, the union with the unseen filter,
and the $5\%$ fleet-share threshold.
Of four shares, each added to the base rule, only $5\%$
produced no more false alarms
than the rule alone on the first three test days.
However, $0.5\%$ blocked about 25 times fewer legitimate
clients and more of a 100-attacker botnet
(Appendix~\ref{app:window}).
The values $\rho = 3$ and $k_{\min} = 5$,
the 99th percentile for $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$,
and the 25-stack botnet were fixed on generated
traffic before any production data were examined.

We report \textbf{collateral damage} as the share
of legitimate traffic within the selected scope:
the false-positive rate of the filter if deployed. On
production traffic, the false-alarm
rate (FA) is the share of clean windows in which a
configuration selects a filter: 5\,643 on the test days and
1\,152 on the held-out day, leaving out windows under
$k_{\min}$ origins. Exact 95\% intervals assume
independent windows, although false alarms cluster by day.
Each alarm's collateral (Coll.) is the share of that
window's clients the filter blocks, given as a median over
alarms. The false-alarm rate times the mean collateral is
the expected share
of clients blocked per clean window.

The tables also give the scope's rate without the gate
(\textit{No gate}), the rate under a $1\,000$-user flash
crowd (\textit{Fl.\,1k}), and the blocked share
(\textit{coverage}) of botnets sized at one median window or
a tenth of it ($1\times$, $0.1\times$).
Coverage averages over all attack windows, including those
the gate misses, so it depends on how often the gate fires,
whether the scope selects the botnet's fingerprints, and
what share of attackers they cover.

On generated traffic, each scope selects sessions without
training or labels (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}), while
per-session detection stays at chance
(Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). The modal scope fails as
attacks span more stacks. With one
botnet stack, the modal fingerprint and binomial test both
cover $89.8\%$ of attackers, with no observed collateral.
From five stacks onward, as Section~\ref{sec:evidence}
predicts, the modal fingerprint belongs to legitimate
clients. It covers $0.0\%$ of attackers and blocks $39.0\%$
of legitimate traffic, rising to $61.1\%$ under a more
concentrated legitimate population ($\alpha = 2.0$).

Profile-relative scopes perform better. The binomial test
and $z$-score cover $90.0\%$ of attackers at five stacks
and $90.3\%$ at 25, with \textbf{no observed collateral}
in these campaigns. The rest use one-off fingerprints, so
scoping trades coverage for precision. At 100 stacks, each
holds about ten attackers, too few to stand out against a
1\,000-session profile, so the test falls well below the
$z$-score and unseen filter (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}).
Pooling the
profile over 30 attack-free periods restores coverage
to $89.6\%$. The unseen filter matches or exceeds the test,
with mean collateral $0.03\%$ (at most $0.5\%$ in one seed),
because new stacks never occur in generated legitimate
traffic.
It cannot select shared stacks, which also occur
among legitimate clients. For those, the test covers
$85.4\%$ of attackers at $2.23\%$ collateral,
versus $89.9\%$ at $3.37\%$ for the $z$-score.

The learned model needs labels unavailable to an operator
during an attack. At 25 stacks and zero false positives, the
test is far ahead. With $\log b(f)$ and log
enrichment per session, the model recovers most of the
attack at 25 and 100 stacks (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic}),
although it trains on labels from the campaign it scores.
At $1\%$ false positives, which the test does not need, (d)
reaches $66.8\%$ at 25 stacks, or $94.6\%$ with the profile.

\textbf{Two conditions limit these results.}
When attackers adopt common legitimate fingerprints,
outside the threat model, little is enriched. The test
covers $30.4\%$ of attackers
at $3.78\%$ collateral, and the $z$-score $57.3\%$ at
$11.3\%$. The modal scope covers $3.6\%$ at $39.0\%$, and
the learned model $23.4\%$ at $1\%$ false positives. A flat
or missing profile makes every fingerprint appear rare and
increases
collateral to $77.6\%$, whereas a profile drifted
to $\alpha = 2.0$ has little effect. Per window, $\Omega
\geq \tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ alone also fires on flash
crowds~\cite{jung2002flash}. The test removes these alarms
for generated crowds (Appendix~\ref{app:window}), though not
all of them for crowds of production clients, as shown next.

\textbf{False alarms.}
Pooled across endpoints, the calibrated scope and
distinct-origin gate each exceed the $1\%$ budget alone on
test days, and only together stay below it
(Table~\ref{tab:production}). The binomial scope alone
selects a filter in $2.2\%$ of clean windows, while
the gate opens in $3.0\%$, reaching $5.4\%$ on the
busiest endpoint. Together, they misfire in only
$0.1\%$ of clean windows (5 of 5\,643; 95\% interval
$0.03$--$0.21\%$), including four on the web console.
The five joint misfires, versus 3.5 expected under
independence, are compatible with chance
($P = 0.27$, Appendix~\ref{app:window}).
The gate's other 163 openings ($2.9\%$) go to the fallback,
a rate limit or a challenge to every client. The five
misfires block a median $32.9\%$ of clients in their
windows,
or an expected $0.03\%$ per clean window.
Flash crowds of 1\,000 production clients trigger
the configuration in $22.2\%$ of windows, with lighter
filters blocking a median $4.3\%$ of the window's clients.

\textbf{What the scope can select: the calibration floor.}
The strict level that keeps false alarms rare also raises the floor.
On the busiest endpoint, legitimate fleets push $\lambda_e$
to $10^{-60}$, and exempting five or six
known fleets raises it to about $10^{-21}$.
For a 25-stack botnet on new fingerprints, the unseen filter
sets the floor on E1--E3 at 139 attackers per window, where
the average stack
reaches $k_{\min}$ origins. On E4 the test selects stacks
first, from 84 attackers
(Table~\ref{tab:floor}). For shared stacks, all beyond the
profile's ten most common fingerprints and mostly rare, the
calibrated level sets the floor, computed at their median
share. A typical stack
needs 254 attackers on the busiest endpoint, $8\%$ of its
median window, and 4 to 19 median windows on the smaller
endpoints, as medians of daily ratios.

The shared-stack floor grows with the number of stacks, from
$2\%$ of E1's median window at five stacks to $34\%$ at 100.
It also increases with fingerprint popularity:
ranks 11--35 are never selected on the two
largest endpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).
The floor is not a sharp threshold: below it, chance leaves
some stacks large enough to be selected. A binomial model
of stack sizes predicts $43.1\%$ coverage without the gate
for 100 new-stack attackers on E1, close to the measured
$43.2\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:stops}).

\textbf{Beyond the enrichment limit, selection is unlikely.}
For a 25-stack botnet, the 4--28 most common fingerprints of
each endpoint, carrying $86$--$94\%$ of its origins, exceed
the limit. A botnet using the endpoint's
25 most common fingerprints, outside the threat model, is
therefore largely
missed. The configuration blocks $7.0\%$
of 100 attackers and $23.8\%$ of 1\,000.
It blocks none on the two largest endpoints,
where nearly all 25 fingerprints exceed
the limit, but $73.5\%$ of 1\,000 on single sign-on, where
only four or five do.

\textbf{Other triggers.}
To locate the loss, we evaluated two other triggers, both
exploratory, after the held-out day was read
(Table~\ref{tab:triggers}, with the binomial in sample, as
the protocol fixed it). A \textit{seasonal gate} divides the
distinct origins by their median at the same hour of the
calibration days and fires at that ratio's 99th percentile.
It improves coverage of tenth-size
botnets on the busiest endpoint.
Behind it, however, only the cross-fitted beta-binomial and
unseen filter stay within the budget on the web console,
where the binomial and in-sample beta-binomial misfire in
$2.0$--$2.6\%$ of windows.
The scope without a gate (\textit{No gate}, dashed in
Fig.~\ref{fig:stops}) covers about $90\%$ of E1's tenth-size
botnet on new stacks each day.
With known fleets, the binomial scope alone sits at the
budget on E1, rising from none on the first two test days to
eight of 288 on the last.

\textbf{What the unseen filter and test contribute.}
The unseen filter selects new stacks of at least $k_{\min}$
origins, and the test adds shared stacks
and, in some configurations, smaller new
ones. For larger new stacks, including
E1's tenth-size botnet, the unseen filter
matches the test under every trigger
with fewer false alarms and flash-crowd firings. Its false alarms
without a gate cluster by day
(Appendix~\ref{app:window}).
For smaller new stacks, the cross-fitted
beta-binomial adds coverage on the smaller
endpoints and, without a gate, on the busiest one ($58\%$
versus the filter's $43\%$ of 100 attackers). The binomial
adds coverage only on single sign-on.
With the seasonal gate, the cross-fitted
beta-binomial covers $53$--$84\%$
of 100 new-stack attackers on the smaller endpoints, but
$3.9\%$ on the busiest.

\subsection{What the Evidence Supports}
\label{sec:posthoc}
\label{sec:boundary}

\textbf{Post hoc analyses.}
Among calibrated scopes used without a gate, only the cross-fitted
beta-binomial approaches the $1\%$
budget ($1.1\%$, Fig.~\ref{fig:operating}).
In sample, the beta-binomial lowers
the shared-stack floor on the three
largest endpoints (Table~\ref{tab:floor}),
but 19 of its 22 false alarms occur
on the web console, where it fires on $74.4\%$ of 1\,000-user crowds.
An initial cross-fitting design,
calibrated only on the last calibration
day, was dropped after its results were seen, because one
day misses the rare fleet events that set the level.
Under cross-fitting, the binomial configuration barely
changes, with six false alarms versus five, and the
calibrated $z$-score loses its coverage lead
(Fig.~\ref{fig:operating}).

\textbf{Prospectively, false alarms increase.}
On the held-out day, the binomial
configuration produces two false alarms
in 1\,152 clean windows, consistent
with the test-day rate ($P = 0.27$).
The gate opens in only three windows, and the scope misfires
in two, both on the web console, more than the test days'
five of 168 openings predict (post hoc, $P = 0.003$). The
14-day block was exported after its protocol became public
and analyzed after
publication of its export hashes
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
There the configuration misfires in 31 of 16\,103 clean
windows ($0.19\%$;
95\% interval $0.13$--$0.27\%$,
Table~\ref{tab:block}). This is about twice the test-day
rate and, by the protocol's test, inconsistent with it ($P <
10^{-4}$), though below the $1\%$ budget. The gate
opens about half as often, but the
scope misfires in 31 of 233 openings
($13\%$), versus five of 168 on test days ($P < 10^{-11}$).
Of these misfires, 30 occur on weekdays and all on the smaller
endpoints, 17 in the console's 29 gate openings.
The selected filters block a median $39\%$ of those windows' clients.

\textbf{The pre-specified comparison favors the seasonal gate.}
Behind it, the cross-fitted beta-binomial stays within the
budget everywhere ($0.05$--$0.35\%$) and is preferred on
every endpoint to the unseen filter without a gate. On the
smaller endpoints, it covers $68$--$88\%$ of 100
new-stack attackers, versus $43\%$ for the unseen filter,
and $34$--$66\%$ on shared stacks,
which the unseen filter cannot select.
On the busiest endpoint, its advantage
comes only from shared stacks:
it adds $11.4\%$ coverage of the
tenth-size botnet, but covers just $11.8\%$ of it on new
stacks, versus $89.6\%$ for the filter. The unseen filter
produces no false alarms, while
the binomial scope with known fleets
exceeds the budget there without a gate ($1.56\%$).

\textbf{This WAF's verdicts cannot serve as labels.} The
operator's WAF, the only source of labels at hand,
blocks a median $9$--$63\%$ of
clients per window on E2--E4,
and none on E1. On windows that keep blocked clients, a
profile without them makes the scopes reproduce WAF
decisions by construction: a stable fingerprint is enriched
only if $70$--$88\%$ of its clients were blocked. With a
profile of all clients, only $0.4$--$33\%$ of the clients
the three calibrated scopes would block were also blocked by
the WAF, below the $10$--$63\%$ of a random pick on every
endpoint. On the API
and single sign-on, the scopes catch at most two of the
WAF's 38 surges (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).

\textbf{Which configuration to recommend.}
Two factors guide the choice for each endpoint:
the calibration floor and the trigger's sensitivity.
On the three smaller endpoints, the cross-fitted
beta-binomial selects shared stacks only above
3 to 4 median windows and new stacks from 2 to 3. Our
current candidate is the cross-fitted
beta-binomial behind the seasonal gate, with the fallback
below its floor. Although developed post hoc, it was fixed
before the 14-day block, where the pre-specified comparison
preferred it to the unseen filter on every endpoint. There
it produced fewer
false alarms than the
binomial configuration (20 versus 31, though 14 versus none
on the busiest endpoint) and stopped more of 100 attackers.
Crowds of 1\,000 users triggered it in one third
of windows, generally with lighter filters,
while its misfires blocked a median two thirds of clients in
their windows. On its gate's other clean openings ($1.5\%$
of windows), the fallback would act, a cost not included in
the false-alarm budget. The calibrated $z$-score misfired as
often, with fewer crowd activations but lower attack
coverage.
On the busiest endpoint, the beta-binomial
misses most new-stack attackers, whereas the unseen filter
without a gate retains high coverage with no false alarm on
the block.
Running both there is untested.

The next step is a longer evaluation, since no later day has
tested this choice.
The binomial configuration's misfires occur
mainly on weekdays, suggesting a gate calibrated by day of
the week may help.
The beta-binomial's misfires also cluster
on two days of the busiest endpoint.
Whether an attack near the floor exhausts a service depends on its spare
capacity, which we did not measure.

\textbf{What each evaluation shows.}
The generator isolates the selection mechanism
under mimicry imposed by design.
Its campaigns, a third to half of the origins in the windows
they overlap, are easier than the tenth-size injections
where the production gate mostly fails. Its new stacks
never occur in legitimate traffic, which is why the unseen
filter matches the test there. Production traffic
measures false alarms on real clients, but
attack coverage only for injected botnets
with a fixed 25-stack structure: new stacks,
mostly rare shared stacks, or the endpoint's
most common fingerprints. The 23 days cover one operator's
own endpoints, too few to calibrate a weekly cycle.
The five choices in Section~\ref{sec:configs}
were made on the reported test days,
so only the held-out day and the 14-day
block evaluate them prospectively.
Cross-fitting removes the in-sample fitting
of calibration levels but does not undo
earlier configuration choices.
Appendix~\ref{app:limitations} lists further limits.

\textbf{The scope requires distinguishable fingerprints.}
A bot outside the threat model that uses
a common legitimate fingerprint, or a
different fingerprint on every device,
evades selection (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
An alarm then mainly invokes the fallback. Encrypted Client
Hello~\cite{ietf2026ech} does not prevent
a defender terminating TLS from observing
the inner \textit{ClientHello}, as assumed
here. Profiles must also remain current:
a software release that introduces a new
fingerprint for many legitimate clients
would immediately trip the unseen filter, although
we observed no such event over 23 days.
We did not inject flash crowds concentrated
on a single client type, which could be
harder to distinguish, or attackers that
manipulate the profile or fleet exemptions.
Profiles should therefore be rebuilt from windows where the
scope selects nothing, while
persistent new fingerprints should enter
the profile only after operator review.

\textbf{What exchange loses, and what the ontology adds.}
Of the standards examined, STIX~2.1, DOTS and BGP Flowspec
define no JA4 property in their core vocabularies.
OCSF records JA4 fingerprints in network
events and finding evidence~\cite{ocsf2025schema} but
defines no filter or remediation over them. The MISP
importer
drops fingerprints carried by a STIX~2.1
extension because it does not map them
to MISP's JA4 object. A 2025 extension
of the ACL model supports matching TLS
client parameters but does not define
a fingerprint match~\cite{ietf2025mudtls}.
DOTS reuses that model~\cite{dots2020data} without adopting
the extension. Exchanging fingerprint rules would therefore
need a JA4 match in DOTS filters and a JA4
property in STIX. The ontology does not
improve detection accuracy or trigger
sensitivity. It is the deployable specification of the
evidence and selected scope, compiled into log-store queries
that reproduced the
evaluated exports' address and /24-pair
counts, and JA4-pair counts on the endpoint where the WAF
blocked no client
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Calibrated TLS-fingerprint scoping, a test on
ontology-specified counts, provides selective mitigation
when attack fingerprints are rare among
legitimate clients, botnets pass the floor, and the trigger
fires. On generated stealthy campaigns,
profile-relative selection blocks $90\%$ of attackers on up
to 25 new stacks, without labels or observed collateral.
Selecting the alarm's most common fingerprint instead blocks
legitimate clients once
the botnet spans several stacks. On production CDN traffic,
legitimate fleets impose a calibration floor:
a 25-stack botnet on rare shared fingerprints needs about
$8\%$ of the busiest endpoint's median window,
or 4 to 19 median windows on the smaller endpoints, before
the binomial test selects them.

The binomial configuration misfires in $0.1\%$ of clean
windows on the days used to configure it, although
its scope alone exceeds the $1\%$ budget ($2.2\%$).
Across 14 later pre-specified days, the scope misfires in
$13\%$ of gate openings, so false alarms double to $0.19\%$,
still within budget. Two of three post hoc configurations
also stay within it.
On the busiest endpoint, the gate misses most of a
tenth-size botnet. Without a gate, the unseen filter blocks
about $90\%$ of it on new stacks, with no false alarm on the
14 days. On E1, the cross-fitted beta-binomial behind a
seasonal gate, preferred to the filter everywhere, adds
shared stacks but loses most new ones.
Future work includes weekday-aware calibration, HTTP/2
attacks and captured stealthy campaigns.

\section{Sub-Relation Specification}
\label{app:algorithms}

The rule's coordination mass is \begin{equation} \Omega(S) \;=\; \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{rel}}} w_i \,\big|E_i(S)\big| , \label{eq:omega} \end{equation} where $E_i(S)$ is the set of pairs of distinct origins $\{o(s_a), o(s_b)\}$ linked by sub-relation $i$, with $o(s)$ the source address of session $s$. Each pair counts once, so one client's many connections cannot mimic coordination. The weight $w_i$ follows the attacker's cost of breaking the signal, from the TLS fingerprint ($1.0$) through endpoint convergence ($0.6$) to network proximity ($0.3$), since carrier-grade NAT makes a shared /24 a weak discriminator, and calibration supports only the top of that order (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}). For an equality sub-relation, $|E_i(S)| = \sum_k \binom{n_k}{2}$ over class sizes $n_k$ in distinct addresses, so $\Omega$ depends only on the class sizes returned by the query (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}). The production gate and endpoint term add plain-HTTP clients to the $n$ of Section~\ref{sec:rule}, which counts an address once per fingerprint. The compiled query counts each address once.

\textbf{\texttt{TLSFingerprint}:} exact equality of the full JA4, the relation
exercised here. A near variant, \mbox{\textit{ja4\_distance = 1}}, would absorb version drift within one client library, but is not an equivalence relation and would require pair edges, so it is specified but not exercised. \textbf{\texttt{ReusedIdentity}}, \textbf{\texttt{TemporalPattern}} and \textbf{\texttt{PayloadSignature}}, also specified but not exercised, match a shared cookie, token or username, inter-arrival sequences within a DTW distance $\tau_{\mathrm{DTW}}$, and payload summaries above a cosine similarity $\tau_{\mathrm{payload}}$. \textbf{\texttt{EndpointConvergence}:} same endpoint, here a host or an address and port, with path-pattern normalization specified but not exercised. \textbf{\texttt{NetworkProximity}:} shared IPv4 /24 or IPv6 /48, with a shared ASN specified but not exercised.

\section{Weight Calibration and Generator Inputs}
\label{app:calibration}

On scenarios from earlier runs, a near-uniform benign vocabulary against a monolithic botnet, maximizing the per-session ROC AUC of $w_{\mathrm{tls}}\,z(\mathrm{ja4}) + w_{\mathrm{ep}}\,z(\mathrm{endpoint\ conv.}) + w_{\mathrm{net}}\,z(\mathrm{net\ prox.})$ over the grid $\{0.3\ldots1.0\}^3$ (125 combinations, 60 scenarios), yields $(1.0,\ 0.3,\ 0.3)$. Only the TLS fingerprint discriminates alone (AUC $0.93$, versus $0.50$ for endpoint convergence and $0.58$ for network proximity), and every endpoint weight in the grid reaches the same optimal AUC of $0.943$, as does the ontology's $(1.0,\ 0.6,\ 0.3)$. Thus only the top of the order is identified, and the per-window rule performs equally well with uniform weights (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). Request counts come from all $322\,658$ benign CICIDS2017 sessions and durations from the $40\,351$ with two or more requests. The default pool of $2\,000$ benign fingerprints follows the Zipf shape of the $495$ measured at one point of presence and exceeds the $76$--$1\,163$ observed per production endpoint. Popularity is measured per request, which if anything overstates concentration.

\section{Additional Experiments and Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\textbf{Instrumentation and deployment.} Counting origins by source address undercounts users behind carrier-grade NAT and overcounts clients rotating IPv6 privacy addresses, whose natural unit is the /64. The production run is offline, over counts per endpoint, so a live CDN deployment still needs the partitioning of Appendix~\ref{app:cost}, not implemented here.

\textbf{Coverage.} HTTP/2 attacks and other application protocols (DNS, gRPC) require sub-relations beyond the Slow HTTP DoS family over HTTP/1.1, and $\Omega(S)$ instantiates only the three sub-relations exposed by the generator and captures, leaving the others' weights untested. We found no independent capture of the stealthy regime: BCCC-cPacket-Cloud-DDoS-2024 criticizes the application-layer coverage of earlier datasets and provides 17 TCP-based DDoS scenarios~\cite{shafi2024bccc}.


\textbf{Laboratory captures and KLAGE.} CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} (Slowloris, Slowhttptest, HULK, GoldenEye) and CIC-IoT2023~\cite{neto2023ciciot} (DDoS Slowloris, HTTP Flood), on which KLAGE reports $F_1 = 0.841$ for \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, run through the same extraction pipeline (PCAP $\rightarrow$ JA4 + flows $\rightarrow$ sessions). On CIC-IoT2023 \texttt{DDoS Slowloris}, whose attack clusters contain seven origins in one of 143 /24 prefixes, a strong per-session baseline using nine flow attributes reaches $F_1 = 0.900$ (AUC $0.987$), while three of them plus cross-session evidence reach $0.911$ (AUC $0.982$). Real Slowloris, unlike our stealthy campaigns, leaves a per-session flow signature. The comparison with KLAGE is \textit{uncontrolled}: our strong baseline also exceeds the published $0.841$, and the released code starts from a graph of unpublished construction and provides no weights, preventing a controlled rerun.

\textbf{Per-session detection and learned models.} Each configuration, (a) to (d), runs on 30 seeds (Table~\ref{tab:symbolic} uses 15), with each campaign split 70/30 into training and test sessions. Configuration (a) uses only the nine per-session flow attributes in a Random Forest (RF), repeated with gradient boosting, a multilayer perceptron and logistic regression. Configuration (b) adds per-session ontological attributes, (c) adds only network proximity, and (d) adds cross-session evidence from the three sub-relations: the share of the cluster sharing one JA4 or /24 plus its size. We reimplement PCA profiling~\cite{fernandes2015autonomous}, PCA with $k$-means over a behavioral matrix~\cite{bharathi2012pca} and a supervised RF/SVM over session features, after~\cite{kemp2023approach}. At $K = 50$ and $K = 1000$, (a) reaches a per-session ROC AUC of $0.498$ and $0.503$, (b) $0.500$ and $0.502$, the three academic baselines $0.495$--$0.518$, and (a) stays at $0.488$--$0.503$ under every classifier family ($\alpha = 1.5$, $M = 25$, 30 seeds), the chance level the stealth mode imposes. The cross-session representation (d) reaches $0.927$ and $0.982$, while (c) reaches $0.499$ and $0.659$ (paired Wilcoxon tests, Bonferroni-corrected, $p_{\mathrm{Bonf}} = 7.5\times 10^{-9}$ for (d)$-$(c) and (d)$-$(a) at $K = 1000$).

\textbf{Learned representation.} That separability does not transfer. Fragmenting the botnet across $M$ stacks makes cross-session evidence non-monotonic in the label: at $K = 1000$, an attacker shares a fingerprint with a few dozen peers, while a legitimate client in the benign head shares one with hundreds. Trained on five stacks and tested on 25 or 100, (d) falls to $0.61$ and $0.63$, and trained on 25 it falls to $0.48$ on five and $0.75$ on 100, versus $0.95$--$0.995$ on the same $M$ with other seeds. Cross-$M$ tests use disjoint seeds because the generator draws every benign session before any $M$-dependent step: a same-seed split would score (a) at $0.88$--$0.94$ by memorization.

\section{The Rule per Window and Production Details}
\label{app:window}

Here the rule runs as deployed, per $W = 300$~s window and endpoint, and no threshold sees an attack or label. $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ is the 99th
percentile of $\Omega$ over the windows of 30 attack-free runs, which also form the profile. False alarms are counted on 30 more runs, detection on default campaigns ($\alpha = 1.5$, $M = 25$) over windows with at least $k_{\min} = 5$ attackers, and a flash crowd retimes 25, 50 or 100 legitimate sessions from a third run into one window. We spread benign starts over 60~min as the generator otherwise packs them early.

Over 30 runs each, $\Omega$ passes $\tau_{\mathrm{cluster}}$ in $77.8\%$ and $85.7\%$ of the 90 and 35 attack windows at $K = 1000$ and $50$, in $0.6\%$ of 360 clean windows and in $80$--$100\%$ of the flash crowds of 25 to 100 users. A flash crowd adds mass as a campaign does. The test separates them because generated crowds draw users from the benign mix the profile describes, whereas a surge on one client type is enriched, as production fleets show. With the test, the rule fires on no clean window (one-sided $95\%$ upper bound $0.8\%$) and no flash crowd, selects a filter in every attack window flagged by $\Omega$ and blocks a median $75$--$80\%$ of attackers, with no legitimate session hit. With uniform weights $\Omega$ catches at least as many attack windows
($78.9\%$ and $85.7\%$), and without its endpoint term $33.3\%$ and none, while flagging $53$--$100\%$ of flash crowds. A distinct-origin gate catches $82.2\%$ and $85.7\%$, and with the test no clean window or
flash crowd.

\textbf{Production traffic.} The exports contain no prefixes, so the scope is judged on JA4 alone. At level $0.01$, it selects a filter in $60.1\%$ of clean windows when counted in connections, since one client opens many, and $26.9\%$ when counted in origins, because legitimate fleets switch on together. Calibrating $\lambda_e$ on the preceding days brings the base rule to $0.2\%$. The distinct-origin gate, which fires with $\Omega$ in 136 of the 181 clean windows where either fires, halves the base rule's false alarms with no loss of detection. At $\lambda_e = 10^{-60}$ the binomial test selects a stack only past 15 to 18 origins. Fleets, such as a probe fleet active only in business hours, push $\lambda_e$ down, and without fleets exempted the test alone selects a filter in $1.5$--$6.3\%$ of the next day's windows, often a fleet blocking about half of a window's clients.

\textbf{Details of V-B.} Windows with fewer than $k_{\min}$ origins, where no gate can open, are left out: 117 of E4's 1\,440 test-day windows, none on the held-out day. In attack windows the calibrated scopes block a median $0\%$ of legitimate clients and up to $71\%$ in the worst window of a small endpoint. Gate and scope misfire together more often than independence predicts for the post hoc beta-binomial and the calibrated $z$-score, in 22 and 20 windows versus 7.5 and 11.9 ($P < 10^{-4}$ and $P = 0.02$ for a Poisson count), and in 6 and 13 versus 1.8 and 4.6 cross-fitted. For the binomial configuration the excess is within chance, 5 versus 3.5 and 6 versus 3.2 cross-fitted ($P = 0.27$ and $0.10$). The expectation pools days per endpoint, so day-to-day variation alone can inflate the excess. The misfires of the other two also cluster by day, 13 of each on one day of the console, so exact intervals, which assume independent windows, understate their uncertainty. Under the binomial in sample, the shared-stack floor for 5 and 100 stacks is 50 and 1\,026 attackers on E1, 164 and 6\,158 on E2, 28 and 1\,017 on E3 and 17 and 454 on E4. For 25 stacks, fingerprints of rank 11 to 35 need 808 on E3 and 114 on E4, and those of rank 36 to 100 need 743, 1\,898, 227 and 84.

\textbf{Details of V-C.} Pooled, the binomial configuration stops $38.4\%$ and $77.7\%$ of 100 and 1\,000 attackers on new stacks and $21.7\%$ and $63.3\%$ on shared ones, figures the small endpoints dominate. On the busiest endpoint, depending on the day, the gate opens in $0.7$--$42\%$ of the windows of a botnet a tenth the size of the window. It opens in $45\%$ at a third and in $79\%$ at a whole window. Of 100 attackers on new stacks, the unseen filter by itself behind the origin gate stops $29.4\%$, the calibrated $z$-score $54.7\%$ ($23.7\%$ on shared ones) and the post hoc beta-binomial $59.8\%$ ($40.5\%$). Fingerprints absent from the profile appear daily but rarely concentrate: only $0.21\%$ of test-day windows contain one with at least $k_{\min}$ origins. The unseen filter's misfires as its own trigger cluster by day: all 4 on the busiest endpoint and both on the console fall on one day, and 5 of the API's 6 on another. On each small endpoint, every scope in Table~\ref{tab:triggers} stops $90.0\%$ of 1\,000 attackers on new stacks under every trigger, on both test and held-out days.

\textbf{Details of V-D.} On the block, the configuration misfired in 0 to 6 windows a day, never on the busiest endpoint, and the base rule fired on 30 of those 31 windows. Gate and scope misfired together in 31 windows versus 2.9 if independent, and the scope misfired in 10 of the API's 98 gate openings and 4 of the single sign-on's 95. Post hoc, counting the test days' own uncertainty, a one-sided Fisher test gives $P = 0.07$ for the rate's rise and $P = 0.0002$ for the misfires once the gate opens. The $z$-score reference misfired less (20 versus 31), fired on fewer crowds and stopped more of 100 attackers. Crowds of 100 users trigger the configuration in $3.4\%$ of windows, and its misfires block an expected $0.08\%$ of clients per clean window. On the busiest endpoint the origin gate opens in $0.0$--$13.9\%$ of a tenth-size botnet's windows, depending on the day, and the configuration stops $0.0$--$12.5\%$ of it. Behind the seasonal gate, 12 of the beta-binomial's 14 misfires on the busiest endpoint fall on two days. No fingerprint absent from the profile reached $k_{\min}$ origins in a block window.

Post hoc, on ranks 36 to 100 the busiest endpoint's floor falls from 743 attackers under the binomial to 480 under the beta-binomial in sample, and rises to 949 cross-fitted. Compared with the binomial in sample, the cross-fitted beta-binomial's floor on those ranks is also higher on the API (255 versus 227), equal on the single sign-on and lower on the console (509 versus 1\,898). The beta-binomial's levels then fall to $10^{-17}$--$10^{-2}$, its scope alone fires in $1.2\%$ of the held-out day's windows, and one fingerprint would be a known fleet on E1 on three of five days, though none qualifies in sample.

Most clients blocked by the WAF shared fingerprints with unblocked clients. With a profile built without blocked clients, $84$--$99\%$ of the clients the scopes would block on the full traffic of the API and single sign-on were also blocked by the WAF. With a profile of all clients, the scopes catch the WAF's surges clearly above chance only on the console, and there with under $1\%$ of blocked clients.

\textbf{Other baselines.} Uncalibrated and behind the $\Omega$ gate, the $z$-score fires on $2.6\%$ of clean windows and $64.5\%$ of 100-user flash crowds ($86.8\%$ of 1\,000-user crowds).

\textbf{Known fleets and the scope as a trigger.} Of the four known-fleet shares, each added to the base rule on the first three test days, $5\%$ was the only one whose false alarms did not exceed those of the rule alone (5 versus 6, the others 12 to 16). Using the rate times the median collateral, a rougher measure than the mean, the $0.5\%$ share blocked about 25 times fewer legitimate clients per clean window on those days ($0.0028\%$ versus $0.070\%$). It also stopped more of a 100-attacker botnet on new stacks ($44.6\%$ versus $30.9\%$). Post hoc, the scope alone as a trigger, with known fleets, fires on $2.2\%$ of pooled clean windows, versus $3.5\%$ for the union without them. With known fleets it stops $61.1\%$ of the busiest endpoint's tenth-size botnet on shared stacks, and its misfires there block a median $1.0\%$ of the clients. The post hoc seasonal gate alone fires on $3.0\%$ of clean test-day windows, as the origin gate does, and the binomial behind it stops $10.0$--$44.7\%$ of E1's tenth-size botnet depending on the day. Setting $\rho = 2$ or $5$ changes no pooled firing rate of the base rule by more than $0.3$ points, no blocked share by more than $4.5$ and no collateral median by more than $7.2$.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The reference implementation is available at
\url{https://github.com/Natanael0liveira/knowledge-graph-ddos-article} under the
MIT license. \texttt{make compile-check stix-ingest} verifies the compiled count query on generated sessions, also after a fourth equality signal is added as four triples. Exported bundles pass through the OASIS STIX~2.1 validator in strict mode, the reference TAXII~2.1 server (medallion 3.0.0) and MISP's importer (misp-stix 2026.9.16). The server returns them unchanged except for the extension definition it fails to serve. The compiled query reproduced exported address and /24-pair counts in every window of two production days, 1\,152 windows each, and JA4 pair counts in the 288 windows of each day on the endpoint where the WAF blocked no client. Elsewhere the export drops clients partly blocked by the WAF, which the compiled query keeps. The protocol named JA4 class sizes, and the check compared their pair counts, which constrain the per-fingerprint counts the scope tests but do not identify them. The query ran in the operator's log store, the artifact keeps only its counts, and the evaluation reads the exports.

The held-out day's protocol was written, with export hashes, before the day was read (\texttt{fresh\_day\_protocol.md}), but committed 17 hours after the stated time, so no external timestamp attests that order. The block's protocol was pushed publicly before any of its days was exported, and the export hashes, with an addendum that adds three metrics, before any result was computed (\texttt{heldout\_block\_protocol.md} and its addenda). Its one amended rule, adopted after the export and before any result was computed, compares the small endpoints on 100 attackers and leaves the verdict unchanged. A few rows of raw counts were seen on screen at export. The repository holds every metric both protocols name, whatever its value (\texttt{production\_tables.json}, \texttt{block\_report.json}). Seeds are fixed, so every generated-traffic table and figure regenerates from its make target. Production tables (\texttt{make production-tables waf-labels}) regenerate from per-window results kept with the operator's data, while \texttt{make audit} recomputes about 250 printed numbers from anonymized summaries in the repository. We intend to submit the artifacts to the Artifact Evaluation track.
